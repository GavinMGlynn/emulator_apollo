## It corrected a rule this project had written down twice

`board/ap_disk.h` and `board/ap_board.c` both stated the AT I/O window's mapping
as `Apollo = 0x040000 + AT × 0x80`. §3.1 gives the general rule:

> "Ten-bit consecutive addresses in the I/O address space are mapped into
> processor address space in **groups of eight bytes**, and each group is
> assigned the first eight bytes of a different, but consecutive, page (1024
> bytes). Thus, the first 1024 addresses in PC AT compatible address space
> (0-3FF) map to **128 physical pages (40000-5FFFF)** in processor address
> space."

So `Apollo = 0x040000 + (AT >> 3) × 1024 + (AT & 7)`. **The two agree exactly at
multiples of eight and diverge within a group** — `AT × 0x80` puts AT `302` at
`058100` where the rule puts it at `058002`.

**And this project's own measurement already agreed with the manual.**
`ETHERNET.md` finding 10 records "every oracle access landing on `058002` and
`058006`" for the EtherLink Plus at AT `300` — byte offsets *inside* a group,
which `AT × 0x80` cannot produce. `ap_3c505_decode` takes a base and computes
`offset = address - base`, which is the rule. **The code was right, the
measurement was right, and only the sentence was a special case presented as the
general one.** Both comments now carry the rule, with the divergence and the
evidence named. No behaviour changed and nothing needed to.

*Worth noting what this did not require*: no run, no instrument, no oracle. A
document already on the shelf, read for the first time.

## Chapter 3, the AT bus, is three tables of modelled hardware

**Table 3-1, I/O address space** — the AT-side addresses of the machine's own
devices: disk controller `1A0-1A7` and `3F0-3F7`, tape controller `200-207`,
**Apollo Token Ring Network Controller-AT `220-23F` and `320-33F`**, SPE serial
line 2 at `2F8-2FF`, 802.3 controller `300-307` with an alternate at `310-317`,
SPE parallel `378-37F`, monochrome graphics `3B0-3BF`, colour graphics
`3D0-3DF`, SPE serial line 1 `3F8-3FF`, everything else customer devices.

**Table 3-2, DN3000/DN4000 physical memory** — and it is the *complement* of
`008778-03`'s Table 2-6 rather than a duplicate: where the technical reference
gives the fine rows inside `000000-03FFFF` ("Reserved for the System" here),
this gives what lies outside — `040000-05FFFF` the I/O address space,
`0A0000-0BFFFF` colour or alternate monochrome graphics, `0C0000-0DFFFF`
alternate monochrome or **Apollo Token Ring at `0D0000-0DFFFF`**, `0E0000-0EFFFF`
alternate colour or a second single-board ring, `100000-8FFFFF` main memory
("on the DN4000, this area is not occupied by main memory"), `C00000-CFFFFF` and
`D00000-DFFFFF` the PC coprocessor and its alternate, `FA0000-FDFFFF` monochrome
graphics.

**Tables 3-3 and 3-4, unit numbers and interrupt priority** for the DN3000 and
DN4000: "The unit number of a PC AT compatible device is **identical with the
Interrupt Request (IRQ) line**. There are 16 possible unit numbers; unit number
0 has the highest priority." Timer, keyboard and two others are marked "used by
the processor and **not available on the bus**". *The OCR interleaves the unit,
priority and device columns, so the individual rows here are owed a page-image
read before any IRQ assignment is taken from them.*

## Appendix D, performance, and what it is and is not a source for

**D.1, DMA bandwidth** — hardware figures: "Bus acquisition time can range from
**100 nanoseconds** (minimum) to **2 microseconds** (typical) to **1
millisecond** (worst case; usually during a ring or disk transfer). Once the
controller acquires the bus, it can transfer data over the bus at a rate of **1
microsecond per 16-bit word**." And a design rule: no more than **20 percent** of
bus bandwidth as a long-term average, and "**no single transfer should take
longer than 10 microseconds**".

**These are a cross-check, not a source to adopt.** This core's DMA timing comes
from the 8237's own datasheet — `INTEL_WALK` records "seven separate states,
each composed of one full clock period" — which is the part's behaviour, where
these are the system's including contention. The resolution order puts the
part's manual first. What the appendix offers is a way to *check* the model: a
modelled transfer that is far from 1 µs per 16-bit word would be worth
explaining. **Not measured yet.**

**D.2, interrupt processing overhead** — Table D-1, microseconds, "observed times
may vary up to 10 percent": device interrupt to the first instruction of the
interrupt routine **125**; enabling the device on return **10**; exit with no
eventcount advance **110**; with an advance and nobody waiting **200**; with
someone waiting **265**. A routine that wakes a waiting process therefore costs
125 + 265 = **390 µs**, and with no user routine at all the system handler's own
advance costs **260** or **325 µs**.

**These are AEGIS's software costs, not the machine's**, and this core models the
hardware. They are recorded because they bound what a *real* DN3000 could do
with interrupts, which is the kind of figure that settles an argument about
whether an emulated rate is plausible.

**D.3** gives `pbu2_$wire`, `pbu2_$map` and `pbu2_$unwire` costs on a DSP80 as
SVC overhead plus a per-page term — `0.909 + 1.091n` milliseconds for the
wire/map/unwire approach against `0.913n` for a permanently wired buffer.

## A document this shelf does not hold

§Related Manuals names "the Domain Personal Workstations and Servers **Hardware
Architecture Handbook (007861)**". **`007861` is not on this shelf** — the shelf
has only `019411-A00`, its *addendum*, which is walked. That is consistent with
`apollo-documentary-universe-is-exhausted`'s finding that no public DN3500
hardware document exists, and it is now a *named* absence rather than an
inferred one: the handbook exists, Apollo cited it, and we do not have it.
