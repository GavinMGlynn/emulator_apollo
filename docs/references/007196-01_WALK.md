## Method

- **The text layer is not usable for facts.** It is OCR of a scan and it
  mangles exactly what matters: `DCD` renders as **`DOD`**, `Check` as
  **`Oheck`**, `IOS` as **`lOS`**, `CTRL/Z` as **`OTRLjZ`**. Every `C` in a
  small-caps identifier is a candidate `O`. Use extraction to *locate* pages,
  read the **page images** for anything that is a fact.
- `pdftoppm -r 150` is enough for this document's body text.

## What the read pages have already settled

The SIO options predict the exact register traffic Phase A measured on a booted
machine — one `OPR` bit set at open (`DTR`, default on), the input port read to
inquire `DCD`, one `OPR` bit cleared, and **no** `IPCR` read at all because
`DCD_ENABLE` defaults off and so change-of-state is never armed. The manual and
the machine agree without either having been fitted to the other. Detail in
`PROJECT_STATUS.md`, *Domain/OS reaches a `login:` prompt*.

### The correction: `SMD_$BW_19L` is *not* this core's monochrome board

The section below, written from seven pages, said "the two monochrome boards are
`SMD_$BW_19L`, the landscape one". **That is wrong and is withdrawn.** SMD-13 and
SMD-22 give both orientations outright: for `SMD_$DRAW_REL_U` and
`SMD_$MOVE_REL_U` the column sum "must be between 0 and 799 for **portrait**
displays, or between 0 and 1023 for **landscape** displays", and the line sum "0
and 1023 for portrait ... 0 and 799 for landscape". So

- **portrait** is 800 wide x 1024 tall
- **landscape** is 1024 wide x 800 tall

and neither is 1280x1024. `SMD_$INQ_DISP_TYPE`'s census has **no monochrome
1280x1024 entry at all** — its only 1280 row is `SMD_$COLOR2_DISPLAY`, a colour
type. `ap_graphics.h`'s `008157` and `010735` are 1280x1024 *monochrome* boards,
and this January 1987 manual does not list them. The honest statement is that the
census predates or omits them, not that they are `SMD_$BW_19L`.

*What survives unchanged*: `SMD_$COLOR3_DISPLAY` is `010104`, the 1024x800
8-plane board, and `SMD_$COLOR4_DISPLAY` is the same geometry at four planes,
which is what the sixteen-entry LUT in that file is for. Those two were the
substance and they were right.

### The 128 KB window decomposes exactly

SMD-17 and SMD-18, on the two font loaders: "the images of all loaded fonts
coexist in the **invisible 28-K byte** portion of display memory. This area is
large enough for about eight small fonts." Put that beside SMD-19's 128 KB map
and the portrait geometry and the arithmetic closes with nothing left over:

    800 x 1024 bits  = 102,400 bytes   visible
    131,072 - 102,400 =  28,672 bytes  = 28 KB exactly, invisible

So the 128 KB this core already carried as "the CPU's 128 KB window" is a
800x1024 screen plus a 28 KB off-screen store, and the error list names the store:
`SMD_$HDM_FULL`, "**hidden display memory** is full", with `SMD_$HDMT_UNLOAD_ERR`
for its table. That is a *derivation of the window's contents*, not just its size.

### Two raster lines at a time, which is the interlace signature

`SMD_$SOFT_SCROLL_U` (SMD-27) "starts horizontal or vertical scrolling on the
screen, **2 raster lines at a time**", replicating the two lines on the boundary
opposite the scroll and not clearing them; SMD-28 adds "because scrolling occurs
two lines at a time, the display driver can 'interlace' other tasks with soft
scrolling". A two-line granularity is what an interlaced display forces, which
makes Table SMD-1's `SMD_$NONINTERLACE` coherent rather than puzzling: the display
these calls target **is** interlaced, and the bit turns it off. `008778-03` Table
11-3 gives the DS3500's 1024x800 as *noninterlaced*, so the two describe different
screens and nothing here contradicts the board manual.

### Smaller facts, each checkable against this core

- **The keyboard cursor is 8 bits wide and 13 bits high** (SMD-21), positioned by
  its lower left point.
- **Four locator buttons**: bit 0 lefthand, 1 middle, 2 righthand, **3 fourth
  button** (SMD-26). `ap_kbd.h`'s relative packet encodes three (`B1_LEFT`,
  `B1_MIDDLE`, `B1_RIGHT`); the fourth belongs to a tablet, which is the same
  device `SIO_$BP_ENABLE` and `TPAD_$HAVE_BITPAD` name.
- `SMD_$WHITE` "makes subsequent vectors **white or green**" (SMD-8) — a
  green-phosphor monochrome tube is in scope.
- `SMD_$NOT_IMPLEMENTED`: "**nonconforming and main memory BLTs are not
  implemented**", so the blitter's mode space is wider than the driver offers.
- `SMD_$NOT_ON_COLOR`: some of these calls are monochrome-only, which matches
  SMD-1's "direct control over black-and-white displays".
- `SMD_$CANT_BORROW_BOTH`, "cannot borrow **both** displays simultaneously" —
  yet every call in the chapter says "currently, the only valid unit number is
  1". A node may have two displays and the interface admits one. Recorded as a
  tension, not resolved.
- The touchpad cursor is **initially disabled**, and three conditions must hold
  for a locator to move it (SMD-32).

### Three documentary errors

- SMD-26's example sets `buttons := 3` and comments "{ the lefthand button is
  down }". Bit 0 is lefthand and bit 1 is middle, so 3 is **two** buttons; 1 is
  what the comment describes.
- `SMD_$WRITE_STRING_U` (SMD-37) gives "valid line values 0 to 792 and valid
  column values 12 to 1019 for a portrait display" — **transposed** against the
  vector calls, where portrait's column runs to 799 and its line to 1023. The
  long axis is called *column* here and *line* three pages earlier.
- `SMD_$QUIT_WHILE_WAITING` and `SMD_$WAIT_QUIT` (SMD-41) are two names with the
  identical gloss, "Quit while waiting".

## `SMD` started — the display census, and a bus error this core cannot raise

PDF 543-546, 553, 558 and 561 read as images 2026-09-07: **7 of the chapter's 41
pages**, chosen by extracting the whole chapter and reading the pages whose text
carries numbers. The rest is owed and the coverage row says which. What the seven
gave is worth landing on its own.

**SMD-16's `SMD_$INQ_DISP_TYPE` is a census of every display an Apollo node can
have**, and it is the naming this project did not have:

| Constant | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| `SMD_$NONE` | No display |
| `SMD_$BW_15P` | Black and white **portrait** |
| `SMD_$BW_19L` | Black and white **landscape** |
| `SMD_$COLOR_DISPLAY` | Color display (1024 x 1024) |
| `SMD_$800_COLOR` | Color display with fewer pixels (1024 x 800) |
| `SMD_$COLOR2_DISPLAY` | Color display (1280x1024x8) |
| `SMD_$COLOR3_DISPLAY` | Color display (1024x800x8) |
| `SMD_$COLOR4_DISPLAY` | Color display (1024x800x4) |

`ap_graphics.h` lists three boards -- `008157` and `010735`, both 1280x1024
monochrome, and `010104`, 1024x800 8-plane colour. `SMD_$COLOR3_DISPLAY` **is**
`010104`; `SMD_$COLOR4_DISPLAY` is the same geometry at four planes, which is
what the sixteen-entry LUT in that file is for; and the two monochrome boards are
`SMD_$BW_19L`, the landscape one. *The operating system names its monochrome
displays by tube and orientation where the hardware documents name them by part
number and resolution*, and this is the row that joins the two.

**SMD-11 fixes the coordinate space at 800 x 1024 -- portrait.**
`SMD_$DRAW_ABS_U` takes a column "in the range 0 to **799**" and a line "in the
range 0 to **1023**", and SMD-1 says the chapter is "direct control over
**black-and-white** displays". So these calls are written for `SMD_$BW_15P`, and
a 1280x1024 landscape screen is outside the range they document. Also stated:
"**no error checking is performed on the arguments, for optimal performance**".

**SMD-19 confirms the 128 KB window this core already had.**
`SMD_$MAP_DISPLAY_U`: "display memory is mapped starting at this address, for the
next **128-K bytes**". `ap_graphics.h`'s header already says "the CPU's 128 KB
window", with which plane it selects marked unmeasured -- so the size is now
cited rather than inferred. (1024x1024 monochrome pixels is 131072 bytes, exactly
that window.)

**And a hardware bus error this core cannot raise.** SMD-19: "be careful not to
access display memory while a bit BLT is underway. Doing so causes the offending
program to abort with a **hardware bus error fault**. To avoid this problem, do
not include `IDONE` in calls to `SMD_$BLT_U` or use `SMD_$EVENT_WAIT_U` to wait
for BLT completion." SMD-2 explains the interval: "by default, the display driver
waits for the BLT to complete before returning ... (this is a **busy** wait) ...
if the display mode includes `IDONE`, control returns to the caller immediately,
and generates the SMD event `SMD_$EVENT_SCROLL_BLT_COMPLETE` when it finishes."

*Unreachable here rather than unimplemented, and the distinction is the point.*
`ap_graphics.h` models the blit as "a CPU write to the image memory is the blit
cycle it really is rather than a store" -- every one of the six modes
`002398-04` p. 12-17 lists has the CPU supplying addresses -- so a blit occupies
no interval this model can observe, and nothing can arrive during one. It becomes
reachable the day the blitter is given its own time, and the fault is recorded
now so that day does not have to rediscover it.

**Table SMD-1's five display-mode bits** (SMD-3): `SMD_$CLRMODE` "fill
destination with a constant"; `SMD_$DECR` "source overlaps destination and x is
being decremented"; `SMD_$IDONE`; `SMD_$BLT`, "required in all calls to
`SMD_$BLT_U`"; and **`SMD_$NONINTERLACE`, "disable hardware interlacing"**. That
last one has no home in `CR1`, whose eight bits are all named in `ap_graphics.h`,
and `008778-03` Table 11-3 gives this machine's display as **1024x800
noninterlaced**. So either the bit belongs to a display type this board is not,
or it lives in `CR3A`/`CR3B`. An open question rather than a gap: nothing
observable on a noninterlaced screen depends on it.

Smaller: `SMD_$BLT_REGS_T` is "a **thirteen-element** array of 2-byte integers"
holding "CS; CD; source and destination Xs, Xe, Ys, and Ye; and the display
mode", which is a driver parameter block rather than the board's sixteen
registers; and borrow-display mode has exactly one valid unit number, **1**,
stated on both SMD-4 and SMD-16.

## `GMF` walked whole — the ink convention, confirmed from the format the screen is dumped into

PDF 107-119, GMF-1 to GMF-13, read as images 2026-09-07. Graphics Map Files are a
bitmap interchange format: six calls that dump a rectangle of display memory to a
file and put one back. Three things in it are about this machine.

**"In the GMF, '1' bits are assumed to mean black."** `GMF_$COPY_PLANE` (GMF-5)
and `GMF_$COPY_SUBPLANE` (GMF-7) both take a `black_or_white` Boolean and both
explain it that way, inverting on the way out when a caller says otherwise.
`ap_graphics_scanout` says the same about display memory -- "a set bit is a
**dark** pixel, the bitmap stores ink, not light" -- and got there from the
hardware. Two levels of the stack, one convention. Noted at the scanout.

**A bitmap's scan line is "usually 64" 16-bit words**, i.e. **1024 bits**, which
is the width of `010104`, the 1024x800 8-plane board in `ap_graphics.h`'s list.
The constraint is stated too: "the width must be at least 1/16 of the specified
x-dim ... if you are storing an area 400 bits wide, the source bitmap must use at
least 25 words to represent each scan line", and `GMF_$BAD_WPL` is the error when
it does not.

**`GMF_$RESTORE_PLANE` clips rather than failing**: "if this area runs off the
right side or the bottom of the screen, the GMF manager restores only the portion
of the stored image that fits", and it requires `GPR_$INIT` to have put the node
in **borrow-display mode** first.

*One documentary error, and the numbers give it away.* `GMF_$MEMORY_T` (GMF-2) is
"a **65535-element array of 131070-byte integers**. An array of two-byte
integers." The two sentences contradict each other in three lines, and 65535 x 2
= 131070 -- so it is 65535 two-byte integers occupying 131070 bytes, and the
element size is the total transposed into it.

## `FPP` walked whole — a third printing of the FPCR and FPSR, and every field matches

PDF 93-104, FPP-1 to FPP-12, read as images 2026-09-07. The Floating Point
Package is six calls over the coprocessor's two control registers, and it draws
both **field by field for an application programmer**.

**FPP-5 is the FPCR.** Enable byte at bits 15-8 -- `BSUN`, `SNAN`, `OPERR`,
`OVFL`, `UNFL`, `DZ`, `INEX2`, `INEX1`; `PREC` at 7-6 with `00` extended, `01`
single, `10` double, `11` reserved; `RND` at 5-4 with `00` to nearest, `01`
toward zero, `10` toward minus infinity, `11` toward plus infinity; 3-0 unused.
**FPP-10 and FPP-11 are the FPSR.** Condition-code byte `N` 27, `Z` 26, `I` 25,
`NAN` 24, with 31-28 zero; quotient byte `S` at 23 and "seven least significant
bits of quotient" at 22-16; exception status byte at 15-8 in the same order as
the enable byte; accrued exception byte `IOP` 7, `OVFL` 6, `UNFL` 5, `DZ` 4,
`INEX` 3, with 2-0 zero.

**Every one matches `ap_m68882_regs.h` and `ap_m68882_regs.c`** -- including the
two shifts, `fpcr >> 6 & 3` for precision and `>> 4 & 3` for rounding, and the
five accrued bits. `[881]`'s figures, `002398-04`'s tables and now an operating
system reference agree, which is as many independent printings as this project
will get for these registers. Noted at the enable-byte enum.

**Three smaller things.**

- `FPP_$SAVE_AREA_T` is "an array of **up to 104 bytes**", and
  `FPP_$SAVE_RESTORE_SIZE` explains the "up to": "the number of bytes varies
  according to the FP machine type". So 104 is a maximum over the floating-point
  hardware Domain/OS supports and **not** the 68882's figure -- whose `FSAVE`
  idle frame is 60 (`$3C`), so this library's area is not a bare state frame.
  Recorded rather than decomposed: the manual does not say what is in it.
- **`FPP_$CONTROL` and `FPP_$STATUS` are "for use only with machines equipped
  with the MC68881 or FPX units"**, and anything else gets
  `FPP_$UNSUPPORTED_FUNCTION`. `FPX` is Apollo's own floating-point accelerator,
  named here beside the Motorola part -- so the operating system distinguishes
  three configurations, and a DN3500 is the first of them.
- *A documentary error*: FPP-10 calls the status register "a bit mask of 32 bits,
  of which only the **low 16 bits** are currently used" and then draws bits **31
  to 16**. The sentence is copied from FPP-4, where the control register's
  diagram does show bits 15-0. The diagrams are right on both pages; the FPSR
  page's prose is not.

## `CTM` walked whole — three palette sizes confirmed from the far side, and a colour map on a monochrome screen

PDF 49-60, CTM-1 to CTM-12, read as images 2026-09-07. The Color Table Manager is
a *resource manager* -- it hands out indices into a shared colour map and counts
users -- so most of it is above the hardware. Three things are not.

**CTM-5 tabulates the palette sizes, and they are this core's exactly.**

| Display Type | Available Pixel Values |
| --- | --- |
| Monochrome | 2 |
| 4-plane color | 16 |
| 8-plane color | 256 |

2^1, 2^4 and 2^8. `ap_graphics.h`'s LUT block holds **sixteen entries, four bits
per gun** for the 4-plane board and `AP_BT458_PALETTE_ENTRIES` is **256**; both
were read off hardware manuals, and the operating system counts the same. Noted
at the LUT.

**The colour a program asks for is wider than the 4-plane board can store.**
`GPR_$COLOR_T` is "a 4-byte integer that specifies values for red, green and
blue" -- one byte a gun -- which the Bt458's triple 8-bit DACs take whole and the
4-plane LUT's four bits must truncate. The manual never says so; it follows from
the two widths, and it means `CTM_$FIND_COLOR`'s distance metric (CTM-8: "the
square root of the sum of the squares of the absolute differences in RGB
intensity", best match rather than first) can land on a different entry on a
4-plane node than on an 8-plane one.

**A monochrome screen has a colour map, and it is one bit.** CTM-5: "even
monochrome displays have 'color maps'. Monochrome color maps control whether the
display is operating in white-on-black or black-on-white ... applications can
invert the display by calling `GPR_$SET_COLOR_MAP` for pixel values zero and one,
specifying the colors white and black. This feature is available in direct,
frame, and borrow mode." A two-entry map of black and white carries exactly one
bit, and `CR1`'s monochrome `INV` -- which this core models -- is that bit. *That
the driver reaches `INV` rather than a register this core does not model is an
inference and is flagged as one at `ap_graphics_scanout`*: nothing here traces
`GPR_$SET_COLOR_MAP` down to a monochrome register, and a boot using the `MONO`
command would show which it writes.

**Useful before reading a boot**: the Display Manager preallocates pixel values
**0, 1 and 7 through 15** and releases 8 through 15 only under the `MONO`
command, so on a 4-plane node values 2 to 6 are what an application gets. Use
counts are 16-bit, zero meaning free, decremented automatically when a program
terminates. Borrow-mode applications get "a separate, private copy of the color
map, which is initialized to default values by `GPR_$INIT`", where direct and
frame mode share the one physical map -- CTM-5 says so outright: "the concept of
a shared color map corresponds to the fact that there is a single physical color
map for the display device".

*One documentary typo*: `CTM_$CAN'T` on CTM-12 advises "use **GMR_**`$FIND_COLOR`"
where it means `CTM_$FIND_COLOR`; `GMR` is not a subsystem this manual has.

## `MTS` walked whole — the cartridge drive from the operating system's side

PDF 303-317, MTS-1 to MTS-15, read as images 2026-09-07. The Streams-Magtape
chapter, and this machine's tape is the cartridge half of it.

**It identifies the drive twice.** `MTS_$DEVICE_T` (MTS-4) names three values --
`MTS_$MT` "magtape device", `MTS_$NOT_REALLY` "not currently supported", and
**`MTS_$CT` "cartridge tape device"** -- and Table MTS-1's `MTS_$VOL_DEVICE_A`
gives the numbers: "type of device: **tfp_$mt = 0** for magtape, **tfp_$ct = 3**
for cartridge". So tape traffic on a DN3500 is the `ct` path at device type 3,
and the operating system reserves a code between them that it does not support.

**And it prints a block length that is not the drive's.** `MTS_$BLOCK_LENGTH_A`
defaults to **2048 bytes** and `MTS_$RECORD_LENGTH_A` to the same, where
`AP_SC499_BLOCK_BYTES` is **512** on the authority of QIC-02 Rev D §4.2. Those
are different layers -- a logical block in a magtape descriptor file, four
physical blocks to one -- and the note is now at the constant, because two
numbers this close together get reconciled by mistake.

**Table MTS-1 in full** (MTS-6 and MTS-7), which is the chapter's substance:
unit 0; labelled **true**, "ANSI labeled volume"; reopen false; close-volume
true; save-volume-position false; volume ID auto-generated, six characters
maximum; owner ID auto, fourteen maximum; file sequence 1, or "cur" for the
current file and "end" for a new one at the end of a labelled volume; record
format **D** of `F` fixed / `D` variable / `S` spanned / `U` undefined; block and
record length 2048; ASCII newline handling **true** -- "strip newlines on write,
supply them on read"; file section 1; generation and generation version 1;
creation and expiration dates auto; and `MTS_$BUFFER_OFFSET_A` 0, "**must be
zero**". `MTS_$LABEL` labels "according to **ANSI x3.27-1978**".

*One documentary inconsistency*: `mts_$file_resvl_a` appears in both
`MTS_$GET_ATTR`'s and `MTS_$SET_ATTR`'s attribute lists (MTS-11, MTS-14) and in
**neither** `MTS_$ATTR_T`'s enumeration (MTS-2, MTS-3) nor Table MTS-1. An
attribute a program may set and get and cannot look up.

*Nothing here is a defect in this core*: the chapter is a file-and-volume
interface above the driver, and the one hardware-facing number it carries, the
device type, is not something the SC-499 reports.

## `TIME`, `TONE` and `TPAD` walked whole — the 262,144 µs tick is printed outright

PDF 649-655, 659-660 and 663-670, read as images 2026-09-07.

### `TIME` — the third and decisive statement of the volume-label tick

`CAL` gave the 4 µs unit and the 1980 epoch. `TIME` gives the rest of the
structure and then the number itself.

**`TIME_$CLOCK_T` has two overlaid views** (TIME-3), and the first names exactly
the field this project decodes: `time_$clockh_t` is `high` at offset 0, "**High
32 bits of the clock**", then `low` at offset 4, "Low 16 bits of the clock". The
second view is `high16` at 0 and `low32` at 2. So the 48-bit clock has a *named
type for its top 32 bits*, and a volume label stores that type rather than a
truncation someone chose.

**And TIME-5 prints the tick.** `TIME_$GET_EC` returns the eventcount keyed
`TIME_$CLOCKH_KEY` -- clock-*high* -- and says "the interval between successive
advances of the eventcount is nominally **262,144 microseconds** (about 0.25
second). The exact interval changes slightly with system load."

That closes a constant this project derived the hard way. `PROJECT_STATUS.md`
records the 262144 µs tick as **calibrated**: fitted to two dates the machines
printed themselves, after a quarter-second tick was tried and found 4.9% out.
Three independent statements in two chapters now say the same thing -- the 4 µs
unit, the 16-bit low half (65536 x 4 µs = 262144 µs exactly), and the number in
print beside the name `clockh`. The calibration was right and the figure is no
longer an inference.

*Also on TIME-7*: the error list names `TIME_$NO_Q_ENTRY` "error from
`TIME_$ADVANCE`" and `TIME_$NOT_FOUND` "entry to be canceled not found" -- two
calls the chapter never describes. It documents `TIME_$CLOCK`, `TIME_$GET_EC` and
`TIME_$WAIT` only. A documentary gap, recorded rather than chased.

### `TONE` — two pages, and the 300 ms auto-off is load-bearing

The whole chapter is one call: `TONE_$TIME (time)`, "makes a tone, the tone
remains on for the time indicated", with `time` a `TIME_$CLOCK_T` -- a 48-bit
count of 4 µs periods. **So the operating system asks for an arbitrary duration
while `002398-04` p. 12-2 has the hardware switch itself off after 300
milliseconds.** A tone longer than that cannot come from one ON sequence; the
driver must re-issue it, which is traffic on SIO line 0 that a boot would show.
`ap_kbd.h` models the auto-off and now says why it cannot be rounded away.

TONE-2 also settles that this machine has the part: "only DOMAIN nodes shipped
after **April 19, 1982** contain a working speaker", and a DN3500 is six years
later.

### `TPAD` — which device each half of a bool means

`TPAD_$DEV_TYPE_T` (TPAD-4) names four locator devices -- `TPAD_$UNKNOWN`,
`TPAD_$HAVE_TOUCHPAD`, `TPAD_$HAVE_MOUSE`, `TPAD_$HAVE_BITPAD` -- and
`TPAD_$INQ_DTYPE` returns the last one used, `UNKNOWN` if none since boot.
`TPAD_$SET_MODE` (TPAD-8) then divides them: the call "applies to the touchpad,
mouse, and bit pad locator devices. Note that the mouse uses only the scale and
hysteresis factors and ignores the other mode settings, **since it is an
inherently relative device**."

`ap_kbd.h` carries `pointing_absolute` as a bool -- "which device is on the
cable" -- decided from `002398-04` §13.3's Mode 2 and Mode 3 packet split. The
two readings agree: `false` is the mouse, `true` is the touchpad or the Bit Pad
One. Two documents at two levels of the stack, one division, and the open item on
an absolute pointing device can now name the devices it is for.

*And a bit pad has a second route in*, which an implementer should know before
choosing: `SIO_$BP_ENABLE` in the `SIO` chapter puts "bit pad input from a
graphics tablet" on a **serial line**, with the driver accumulating points and
suppressing any within plus or minus two in x and y.

Driver behaviour recorded as context rather than as gaps: three cursor modes
(`ABSOLUTE`, `RELATIVE`, `REL_ABS`), a hysteresis "box" defaulting to **5 pixels**
whose value is subtracted from the distance moved, the origin re-established
automatically after the finger is off the pad for more than **one-eighth of a
second**, and `TPAD_$RE_RANGE` recalibrating the raw data range "over the next
**1000** data points ... also done for you at system boot".

## `CAL` walked whole — the 4 µs clock gets a citation, and the year window is 1980-2079

PDF 21-45, CAL-1 to CAL-25, read as images 2026-09-07. The Calendar chapter is
the MC146818 seen from the operating system, and it pays four ways.

**`TIME_$CLOCK_T` is a 48-bit count of 4-microsecond periods since 1980-01-01
00:00**, stated on CAL-19 ("This is the number of 4-microsecond periods that have
elapsed since January 1, 1980, 00:00") and again on CAL-8 ("The system clock value
represents a time in units of 4 microseconds"). **That is the citation for a
figure this project derived by calibration.** `PROJECT_STATUS.md` records "a label
time is the high 32 bits of Apollo's 48-bit 4 µs clock from 1980-01-01 -- a 262144
µs tick", arrived at by fitting two dates the machines themselves printed
(SR10.3's "last recorded time was 2015/09/03 15:47:46 UTC" and SR10.4's
2002-11-27) after a quarter-second tick was tried and found 4.9% out. 65536 x 4 µs
is 262144 µs exactly, so the label holds the top 32 bits of this clock and the
calibration was right. An inference from two observations now has a document
behind it.

**The two-digit year window is 1980-2079.** CAL-10, `CAL_$DECODE_ASCII_DATE`: "If
a year between 80 and 99 is specified, CAL_$DECODE_ASCII_DATE adds 1900 to it
before returning. If a year between 0 and 79 is specified, 2000 is added." That is
*not* a rule for the RTC's year register -- it parses ASCII, and `ap_mc146818.c`
is right to refuse to window a register write ("the part cannot express the
century and this core will not invent a windowing rule for it") -- but it names
the era Domain/OS assumes, and it agrees with what this project measured: the
SR10.4 volume's dates are 2002, which is `02` read under this rule.

**The time zone lives on the boot volume, not in the calendar's RAM.** CAL-24,
`CAL_$WRITE_TIMEZONE`: it "writes the supplied time zone information onto the
logical disk volume from which the operating system was started", and is "invalid
on a diskless node". A negative confirmation of what `ap_calendar.c` models in the
fifty battery-backed bytes: the node's configuration is there and the time zone is
not.

**Domain/OS never needs the part's day-of-week register.** `CAL_$TIMEDATE_REC_T`
(CAL-3) is six integers -- year, month, day, hour (24 hr), minute, second -- with
**no weekday field**, and `CAL_$WEEKDAY` (CAL-23) "computes the day of the week for
any Gregorian date" from year, month and day. So a boot is no evidence at all
about `AP_MC146818_DAY_OF_WEEK`, which this core models and tests from the
datasheet alone. *And the two conventions differ by one*: `CAL_$SUN` through
`CAL_$SAT` have "ordinal values 0 through 6" where `[146818]` numbers Sunday as 1.
Both now have citations, so a later reader will not take one for the other.

**Smaller.** UTC is the basis throughout and local time is UTC plus the stored
offset (`APPLY_LOCAL_OFFSET`, `REMOVE_LOCAL_OFFSET`); a timezone difference is
`[+|-]hour:minute` with the hour 0-12 and the minute 0 or 30, negative west of
Greenwich; `STATUS_$T` is reprinted on CAL-5 **identically to SIO-6**, so the
bit-23 difference with `002398-04` is systematic across this manual rather than
one page's slip; and CAL-20 carries a documentary error -- `CAL_$REMOVE_LOCAL_
OFFSET`'s FORMAT line reads `CAL_$APPLY_LOCAL_OFFSET (clock)`, the previous
call's name left uncorrected.

## The partner table is a check on `ap_mc68681`'s baud table, from the far end of the stack

**The A and B lists are exactly the MC68681's set-1-only and set-2-only rates**,
restricted to the fifteen the operating system exposes. `ap_mc68681.c`'s table
gives set 1 as 50, 110, 134.5, 200, 300, 600, 1200, 1050, 2400, 4800, 7200, 9600,
38.4k and set 2 as 75, 110, 134.5, 150, 300, 600, 1200, 2000, 2400, 4800, 1800,
9600, 19.2k. Intersect the set-1-only rates with the OS's fifteen and the answer
is **{50, 7200}** — the A list. Do the same for set-2-only and it is
**{75, 150, 2000, 19200}** — the B list. Everything the manual calls compatible
(110, 134, 300, 600, 1200, 2400, 4800, 9600) is in both sets.

That is worth more than a copy of the table would be, because it is a **negative**
statement: the operating system is not reprinting the part's rates, it is saying
which pairs cannot coexist, and the answer falls out of `ACR[7]` selecting one set
for both channels of a part. Three levels of the stack now agree — `[68681]` for
the mechanism, `002398-04` p. 7-32 for the driver-facing consequence, and this for
the application-facing one — and this is the only one that could have disagreed in
detail.

*The one rate that fits nowhere is `SIO_$3600`*, which is in neither set. The
manual's silence puts it among the compatible speeds, and the machine-type table
above explains why: this list spans DN400s and DSP80s with other UARTs, so it is
the union of what Domain/OS supports and not what any one part can generate. A
DS3500 cannot produce 3600 baud from the generator at all.

**And `DN3000` is listed as having no partnered lines**, which the hardware does
not obviously allow. This board carries two 2681s; `ACR[7]` is one bit per part;
the firmware programs `CSRA = 66` and `CSRB = 77`, both generator codes rather
than the counter/timer or an external pin — so lines 0 and 1 share a set, and so
do 2 and 3, exactly the pattern the table gives for a `DN460`. Three readings,
and nothing here chooses between them:

- The table is a January 1987 list that was not revised for the DN3000 (the
  DN3500 does not appear in it at all).
- "Partnered" is a statement about **what the driver enforces**, not about the
  hardware: on a DN3000 the OS does not check, and a program that sets
  incompatible rates gets the hardware's behaviour with no error.
- The DN3000's serial hardware differs from what `008778-03` §3.9 describes.

The second is the one that costs this project nothing and explains the row: the
constraint is structural in `ap_mc68681` — one `acr` field per part — so the model
behaves correctly whether or not the operating system guards it. Recorded because
a later reader finding this table will otherwise take "No Partners" for a
statement about the silicon.

## `ERROR` walked whole — the field boundaries printed a third time, and the question it was asked comes back no

PDF 77-90, ERROR-1 to ERROR-14, read as images 2026-09-07. This chapter was taken
first among the operating system's own because it had a live question waiting for
it: `002398-04` p. 68 decomposes a status longword into subsystem, module and
code and then points at "Chapter 4, Error Codes and Messages" **to name them**.
If the naming lived here, `E0007` and every other code this project has read off
a console would decode by table rather than by inference.

**It does not live here, and the chapter says so on its first line.** ERROR-1:
"The ERROR programming calls provide status printing and interpretation. This
section describes their data types and call syntax. **The ERROR calls do not
produce unique error messages.**" The eleven calls are accessors and a text
*lookup* — `$CODE`, `$FAIL`, `$MODULE`, `$SUBSYS` pull the four fields out of a
longword; `$FIND_TEXT` and `$GET_TEXT` return the strings for a code; `$PRINT`,
`$PRINT_NAME`, `$PRINT_FORMAT`, `$INIT_STD_FORMAT` and `$STD_FORMAT` write them.
Where the strings come *from* is never stated: it is a runtime database, not a
printed table. So `002398-04`'s own §4 — already walked, and the row for its p. 73
records `(000E0007) name not found` printed outright — remains the only table of
names this project has, and the pointer from p. 68 was a pointer to a different
manual's chapter 4, not to this one. **The question is closed, negatively.**

### What it does settle: `STATUS_$T`, printed a third time and in four pieces

`007196-01` p. 526 gives the longword as a data-type diagram. This chapter gives
the same boundaries as *prose*, one call at a time, and every one agrees with the
diagram:

| call | ERROR page | what it says |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `ERROR_$FAIL` | ERROR-4 | "The fail bit is **bit number 31**" |
| `ERROR_$SUBSYS` | ERROR-14 | "The subsystem is found in **bits 30 through 24**" |
| `ERROR_$MODULE` | ERROR-8 | "The module is found in **bits 23 through 16**" |
| `ERROR_$CODE` | ERROR-3 | "The module-specific code is the **rightmost 16 bits**" |

**This is where the difference with `002398-04` sits.** That manual splits bit 23
out as `A`, "asynchronous fault; only set during delivery of fault", leaving the
module in 22-16. `007196-01` now says 23-16 twice, in two chapters and two
typographies — but it is still *one manual*, so this is corroboration of a
printing, not a second witness to the hardware. The reading followed is unchanged
(`002398-04` is the Engineering Handbook and the more specific), and the
difference remains invisible for any code with bit 23 clear. What the second
printing does buy is that the 23-16 figure is not a typesetting slip in a
diagram: it is what the manual means.

Each of the four calls carries the same sentence — "This routine is intended for
use by FORTRAN programs that need to check for specific status codes. **Pascal
programs can refer to this component directly.**" So the fields are a packed
record in the operating system's own language and these calls exist for languages
without one.

### Smaller facts, recorded because the walk is a walk

- **`STREAM_$ERROUT` is stream ID 3** (ERROR-7, and again on ERROR-10). The
  standard error stream has a fixed small integer, which is the sort of thing a
  console-capture harness eventually needs.
- **`ERROR_$STRING_T` is an array of up to 80 characters** (ERROR-2), and every
  string this chapter returns is bounded by it — subsystem name, module name,
  diagnostic text, command name and user-supplied name alike. `$GET_TEXT` returns
  them "not blank-filled ... only the number of characters necessary".
- **A zero length is how absence is reported**, and the two lengths are ordered:
  "If the subsystem text length is zero, the status is invalid. If the module text
  length is zero, both the module and code fields are invalid" (ERROR-5, ERROR-6).
- **What a console line looks like when the lookup fails** (ERROR-9, ERROR-12):
  "If the text for any of the three fields is not found, the status code is
  displayed in **hexadecimal**, along with the subsystem and module names, if
  known." That is the shape this project has been reading off its own console for
  months — `Unable to resolve "/sys/node_data" -- E0007` is the name-plus-hex form
  of `ERROR_$PRINT_NAME` with the code text missing, not a different mechanism.
  ERROR-9 also names the command behind it: "**The STCODE command**, which can be
  used to view error messages, uses `ERROR_$PRINT` to output the error text."
- `ERROR_$PRINT_FORMAT` is exactly `ERROR_$INIT_STD_FORMAT` followed by
  `ERROR_$STD_FORMAT` (ERROR-7), the format state is kept **per process level**,
  and both take a VFMT control string — the `VFMT` chapter at PDF 711 is where
  that is defined, so this chapter has a forward dependency the walk will meet.
- A zero `status` omits the dash and error text; a zero command `length` omits the
  command name (ERROR-10). The prefix character "for system messages ... is
  usually a question mark (?)", which is the `?(shell)` prefix Domain/OS prints.

### Two documentary errors, recorded so a later reader does not take them for facts

- **ERROR-2**: "`ERROR_$INTEGER32` — A **2-byte** integer. Possible values are
  integers from **-1 through 2147483647**." Two bytes cannot hold 2147483647; the
  name says 32 and the range says 32. The "2-byte" is the error, and it is the
  same boilerplate line the chapter uses correctly for the genuinely 2-byte
  lengths beside it.
- **ERROR-10**: `stream-id` "is usually `STREAM_$ERROUT` (**Stream ID + 3**)",
  where ERROR-7 has the same parenthesis as "(Stream ID **=** 3)". The `=` is the
  right one.

### Nothing here is implementable, and that is the finding

This is an operating-system API chapter with no register, no timing and no device
behaviour in it. The walk records it as **derived** rather than as a gap: the one
thing it could have given this core — a table of subsystem and module names — it
explicitly does not have, and the four field boundaries it does give were already
implemented in the sense that matters, as the decode this project's status and
plan documents use to read a crash word.

## `ACLM` and `EC2` walked whole — the object behind the 262,144 µs tick

PDF 15-17 and 63-73, read as images 2026-09-07, 188 of 722. Both are operating
system chapters with no device in them; they are recorded because the walk is a
walk, and because one of them puts a shape on something this core already
measures.

### `ACLM` — three pages, two calls, nothing here

ACLM-1 states the chapter's whole scope: "The ACLM (Access Control List Manager)
programming calls control the protected subsystem privilege level ... The ACLM
calls **do not use special data types or produce unique error messages**." The
two calls are `ACLM_$UP` and `ACLM_$DOWN`, which assert and deassert a program's
subsystem-manager rights. The one implementation detail the chapter volunteers —
"calling `ACLM_$UP` increments a **counter** in your process; calling
`ACLM_$DOWN` decrements it ... having a counter instead of a flag ensures that if
one routine enables subsystem manager rights, and calls a routine that enables and
disables them, the calling routine does not inadvertently lose its rights" — is
pure operating system. No register, no timing, no device. **Derived and empty.**

### `EC2` — the eventcount is a six-byte object, and 32 of them per node

The `TIME` chapter already gave the number this project cared about: the
`TIME_$CLOCKH_KEY` eventcount advances every **262,144 µs**. `EC2` gives the thing
being advanced.

`EC2_$EVENTCOUNT_T` (EC2-2) is `value`, a 4-byte integer at offset 0, and
`awaiters`, "reserved for internal use by the EC2 manager", at offset 4 — and
every call that takes one says "this data type is **6 bytes** long", so `awaiters`
is a 2-byte field and the diagram's narrower box means what it looks like. The
value is read with `EC2_$READ` and is "a **positive**, 4-byte integer", so an
eventcount is a monotonically increasing 31-bit counter, not a signed one.

**The ceiling is per node, not per process** (EC2-7, EC2-9): "the total number of
eventcounts in ec-plist lists **in any one node** cannot exceed 32", and
`EC2_$PTR_LIST_T` is "an array of up to 32 pointers". `EC2_$ALWAYS_READY_EC` = 1
is a sentinel that replaces a pointer to mean the event is permanently satisfied.

`EC2_$WAIT` returns the **1-based ordinal** of the satisfied eventcount, "the one
with the smallest subscript" when several are ready at once. The system-defined
eventcounts — the clock's among them — are explicitly **not** initialised or
advanced by these calls: "do not use `EC2_$INIT` to initialize a system-defined
eventcount; the system automatically initializes eventcounts associated with
system events ... use the system call that gets the address of the eventcount you
want to wait on", `TIME_$GET_EC` being exactly that call for the clock. So the
262,144 µs tick is the operating system advancing a counter in its own memory on
the back of a hardware interrupt, which is consistent with a figure that "changes
slightly with system load".

**`EC2_$WAIT` and `EC2_$WAIT_SVC` differ only in how they answer a quit**
(EC2-8, EC2-10): `$WAIT` "only returns when an eventcount advances, regardless of
the asynchronous fault handling setting", where `$WAIT_SVC` returns
`EC2_$WAIT_QUIT`. Recorded because a fault arriving mid-wait is the sort of thing
a boot trace shows and the two calls are otherwise identical.

### And a fourth printing of `STATUS_$T`

EC2-3 reprints the status longword — `fail` bit 31, `subsys` 24-30, `modc`
**16-23**, `code` 0-15 — with a *semantic* gloss the other printings do not
carry: "**fail** — if this bit is set, the error was not within the scope of the
module invoked, but occurred within a **lower-level module**." `002398-04` p. 68
words the same bit as "module couldn't handle error", which agrees. This is the
manual's boilerplate data-types page and appears in every chapter that returns a
status, so the walk will meet it repeatedly; it is noted here once and the
`ERROR` section above is where the field boundaries are argued.

## `IOS` and `IOS_DIR` walked whole — a 44-byte directory entry, and the chapter map corrected

PDF 123-208, read as images 2026-09-08, 274 of 722. Eighty-six PDF pages: the
I/O Switch's 75 and the I/O Switch Directory's 8, with three unnumbered pages
between them.

### The chapter map was wrong by two pages, and here is why

The map built from running footers gives `IOS` **73** pages. It is **75**:
IOS-74 and IOS-75 are at PDF 196 and 197, finishing the error list that IOS-73
leaves in the middle of the alphabet at `IOS_$NAME_REQD`. The footer method
solves `start = page - (n - 1)` and votes on ties; it recovers **starts**
reliably — every one confirmed when its chapter is walked — and it derives
*lengths* from the next chapter's start, which is where it goes wrong. Between
two chapters this manual puts **unnumbered pages**: PDF 198 is blank, **199 is
the chapter's own table-of-contents divider** (`IOS_DIR / Table of Contents`,
listing Data Types at IOS_DIR-2, `$ISA` at 5, `$OPEN` at 6, Errors at 8), and 200
is blank again. Those three carry no `NAME-n` footer, so the arithmetic charged
them to the chapter before.

**The rule for the rest of the walk**: a chapter's start from the map is
trustworthy; its length is not, and the way to find the end is to read until the
footer changes. The counts in the map table above are left as built, with this
correction beside them.

### `IOS` — what an operating-system chapter gave a hardware core

Three things, all cross-checks rather than new mechanism.

**The 1024-byte block, twice.** `IOS_$INQ_FILE_ATTR` (IOS-33) returns `blocks`,
"the number of **1024-byte blocks** that the object occupies", and `IOS_$LOCATE`
(IOS-50, IOS-66) allocates "either the length you specify in data-size, or
**1024 bytes**, whichever is the smaller" when a manager cannot hand back a
pointer. This core's `AP_AWD_SECTOR_BYTES` is **1056** — 1024 of data behind a
32-byte block header, which `002398-04` §2 defines and this project used to
settle `AP_VOLUME_LABEL_BASE`. So the operating system's unit of storage is the
sector's *payload*, stated from the far end of the stack and agreeing to the byte.

**The file time stamps are the volume label's type.** `IOS_$INQ_FILE_ATTR`
returns `dt-created`, `dt-modified` and `dt-used` in **`TIME_$CLOCKH_T`**, "a
4-byte integer" — the same type the `TIME` chapter names for the top 32 bits of
the 48-bit 4 µs clock, and the same one the logical volume label's mount history
stores. A file's time stamp and a volume's are one type, ticking every 262,144 µs.

**`UID_$T` is 8 bytes** (IOS-9) and `XOID_$T` is 16 — `rfu1`, `rfu2`, then the
UID at offset 8. That is the width `002398-04` §2's canned UIDs
(`pv_label_$uid` `00000200,0` and company) are printed in, confirmed as a type.

*Smaller things worth having*: `IOS_$MAX` is **127**, so a process has 128
stream IDs, and `IOS_$NO_STREAM` is `16#7FFF`; `NAME_$PNAME_T` is up to **256**
characters; `IOS_$COND_OPT`'s own example is "**data on an SIO line is not
always available immediately**", which is the mechanism behind a console driver
that polls; IOS-26 says the input pad's eventcount "advances after each carriage
return" in cooked mode and "**after each keystroke**" in raw mode; and IOS-37
predicts the shape of a serial line's manager flags — "if you called
`IOS_$INQ_MGR_FLAGS` specifying a stream open on an SIO line, the set returned
would not include any `IOS_$MF_SEEK` attributes, **since serial lines do not
support seeking**". The standard type UIDs (IOS-12, IOS-47) include
**`SIO_$UID` "serial line descriptor object"** and **`MT_$UID` "magnetic tape
descriptor object"**, so a serial port and a tape drive are objects in the
naming tree like any file.

*Documentary defects, recorded so a later reader does not take them for facts*:
IOS-45's heading is `IOS_$INQ_SHORT_KEY` and its FORMAT line calls the same call
`IOS_$INQ_SHORT_SEEK`; IOS-56 writes "`IOS_$RELPLICATE` is analagous to UNIX
DUP" and IOS-55 writes "REPICATE"; IOS-70 writes "`IOS_$SWTICH`". *Documentary
gaps*: IOS-68 names a manager flag **`IOS_$MF_COND`** that the `IOS_$MGR_FLAG_T`
list on IOS-4/5 does not contain, and the error list names **`IOS_$EXPORT`** and
**`IOS_$OPEN_XP`**, two calls the chapter never describes — the same failure mode
as `TIME`'s `TIME_$ADVANCE`.

### `IOS_DIR` — the directory entry, from the other side of the disk

Eight pages, two calls, and one structure that is worth the chapter.

`IOS_DIR_$ENTRY_T` (IOS_DIR-3) is "the format of a record in an `IOS_DIR` object
(that is, in a directory)":

| offset | field | type | meaning |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0 | `enttype` | 2-byte integer | **1 = a file, 3 = a link** |
| 2 | `entlen` | 2-byte integer | length of the name |
| 4 | `entname` | `name_$name_t`, **32 characters** | the name |
| 36 | `unused1` | 4-byte integer | reserved |
| 40 | `unused2` | 4-byte integer | reserved |

and IOS_DIR-2 prints the total as a constant: **`IOS_DIR_$DIR_ENTRY_SIZE` = 44**.

**`002398-04` §2 p. 39 describes the same 44 bytes from the disk side**, and this
walk record already carries that row: "an entry is a **32-byte name**, then a
name length and an **entry type** — `0` not in use, `1` name/UID pair, `3`
name/link-data pair — then four words that are either the UID or a link-text
description", packed three to a 150-byte entry block. The two agree on the size,
on the 32-byte name field and on the type codes `1` and `3`.

**They do not agree on field order, and the difference is the point.** The
handbook puts the name first and the type after it; the streams view puts the
type first and the name at offset 4. And where the disk record spends its last
eight bytes on **the UID or the link text**, the streams record calls them
"reserved for future use" — because a program reading a directory through
`IOS_$GET` is not entitled to the UID. So this is not a second printing of the
on-disk layout; it is the *presented* record, the same width and the same name
field, with the identifying half withheld. A tool that walks a raw volume must
use `002398-04`'s order, and this chapter is the reason to know there are two.

The `0` "not in use" type is absent here for the same reason: a reader never sees
an unused slot.

*The rest of the chapter*: `IOS_DIR_$ISA` asks whether a stream's object
implements the `IOS_DIR` trait, "and if this trait is implemented, then the
object **must be** a directory"; `IOS_DIR_$OPEN` opens a leaf by name relative to
an already-open directory, explicitly "**not in a subdirectory**". The insert
files are `/sys/ins/ios.ins.lan` then `/sys/ins/ios_dir.ins.lan`, in that order.
`IOS_DIR_$MODE_T` is "reserved for internal use" and never defined. The chapter's
error list is one line, `STATUS_$OK`.

## `IPC` walked whole — the datagram's size, and where `[MAC]`'s "typically" came from

PDF 209-226, read as images 2026-09-08, 292 of 722. Sixteen numbered pages, not
the fifteen the map gave — the same failure as `IOS`, and now with a fix.

### The method upgrade: read the chapter's own table of contents

PDF **209 is the `IPC` chapter's table-of-contents divider**, and it settles the
chapter's extent before a page of it is read: `Data Types IPC-2`, `$CLOSE` 4,
`$CREATE` 5, `$DELETE` 6, `$GET_EC` 7, `$OPEN` 8, `$RCV` 9, `$RESOLVE` 11,
`$SAR` 12, `$SEND` 14, `$WAIT` 15, **`Errors IPC-16`**. The last entry is the
last section, so the chapter runs to at least IPC-16, and IPC-16 is one page of
error list.

Every chapter in this manual has one of these dividers. **It is a better length
source than the footer arithmetic and it is one page to read**, so from here the
walk takes each chapter's start from the map, its length from its own divider,
and confirms both by reading to where the footer changes. `IOS_DIR`'s divider
(PDF 199) said `Errors IOS_DIR-8` and the chapter is 8 pages; `IPC`'s says 16 and
it is 16.

**Refined by `MBX`, 2026-09-08.** The divider's last entry is the last section's
**first** page, not the chapter's last: `MBX`'s says `Errors MBX-33` and the
chapter runs to MBX-35, because that error list is three pages. So the divider
gives a *lower bound* that is exact whenever the final section is one page, and
the end is still confirmed by reading until the footer changes. Both were true of
`IOS_DIR` and `IPC` by coincidence.

### The datagram, and the ring

`IPC_$DATA_T` is "an array of up to **1024** characters. The data portion of an
IPC datagram." `IPC_$HDR_INFO_T` is "an array of up to **128** characters. The
header portion." Every call repeats both figures — `$RCV`, `$SAR` and `$SEND`
each say "can contain up to 128 bytes" and "up to 1024 bytes" of their two
buffers — so the pair is stated seven times over.

**This is where `[MAC]` §2.2.2.3's "typically 1024 bytes" comes from.**
`RING.md`'s finding 26 records the hardware limits: packet data is "0 to 4096
bytes, always even, *typically ... 1024 bytes*", and finding 21 puts the packet
header at 12 bytes plus 0-1012 of header data. The protocol manual gives a range
and calls one value typical without saying why. The operating system's datagram
is **exactly that value** — 1024 of data, and a 128-byte header that fits inside
the header field's 1012 with room to spare. The "typically" is not a convention;
it is `IPC_$DATA_T`.

That is a documentary answer to a question `RING.md` had left as an unexplained
adjective, and it is recorded there as well as here.

### The socket, and its ceilings

- **Eight sockets per node for user programs** (IPC-8): "user programs running on
  a node can open a maximum of **eight** sockets on that node. Only **one**
  program at a time can open any socket."
- **A socket's depth is 1 to 4 datagrams** (IPC-8), and IPC-10 says what a full
  one does: "if the socket queue is full when an incoming datagram arrives, **the
  datagram is lost**." So the loss is at the socket, not on the wire.
- **`IPC_$SOCKET_HANDLE_T` is 20 characters**, and a handle lives in a file: a
  program calls `IPC_$CREATE` to make "a special type of DOMAIN system object
  that is used only for socket handles", `IPC_$OPEN` puts the handle in it, and
  `IPC_$RESOLVE` reads it back. Addressing is by **pathname**, which is the
  chapter's opening sentence — "datagrams addressed to destinations identified
  with **file system names**".
- **Timeouts are in quarter-seconds** (IPC-12, IPC-15): `IPC_$SAR`'s `retry-time`
  and `IPC_$WAIT`'s `wait-time` are both "the number of **quarter-seconds** to
  wait for a reply", a 2-byte integer. That is the `TIME_$CLOCKH_KEY` eventcount's
  own 262,144 µs tick used as the unit of a network timeout — the same quarter
  second, three chapters apart.
- **Delivery is not guaranteed**: "even if `IPC_$SEND` completes successfully,
  there is no guarantee that the datagram will be received ... the programs using
  IPC datagrams are responsible for verifying that datagrams are successfully
  received." A datagram service over a ring that acknowledges in hardware, and the
  acknowledgement is not exposed here.
- **A header-only datagram is cheaper**, twice stated (IPC-13, IPC-14): "when you
  send a datagram that contains less than 128 bytes of information, you can place
  all the information in the header buffer. Then specify the data length as zero.
  **It takes less time to send a datagram that contains only a header.**" Which is
  `RING.md` finding 81's `(***) bit 11 of transmit header count = 1 => data length
  = 0` seen from the application: the hardware has a header-only form and the
  operating system tells programmers to use it.

Error list (IPC-16): `IPC_$NOMORE_SOCKETS`, `NOT_IPC_OBJ`, `NOT_OWNER`,
`RANGE_ERROR`, `SOCKET_ALREADY_OPEN`, `SOCKET_EMPTY`, `SOCKET_NOT_OPEN`,
`TIMEOUT`, `TOO_DEEP`, `TOO_MUCH_DATA` — and **both** `IPC_$OK` and `STATUS_$OK`
are listed for "successful completion", which is a chapter with its own duplicate
of the universal success code.

## `MBX` walked whole — virtual circuits over the same ring, and a duration in clock-high units

PDF 227-263, read as images 2026-09-08, 329 of 722. Thirty-five numbered pages
plus the divider and a blank. `IPC` and `MBX` are the two halves of Domain/OS's
interprocess communication — datagrams and virtual circuits — and reading them
together is what makes either legible.

### The mailbox's constants, and where 1024 turns up for the fourth time

MBX-2 prints the whole set:

| constant | value | what it bounds |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `MBX_$CHN_MAX` | **255** | channels open to one mailbox |
| `MBX_$FIL_MAX` | **257 × 32768** | maximum mailbox size, printed as the product |
| `MBX_$MIN_CHN_SIZE` | **64** | minimum channel buffer |
| `MBX_$MSG_MAX` | **1024** | a mailbox message |
| `MBX_$MSG_WMAX` | **512** | the same in words |
| `MBX_$REC_DATA_MAX` | **32760** | data portion of a message |
| `MBX_$REC_MSG_MAX` | **32766** | a server message, header included |
| `MBX_$SERV_MSG_HDR_LEN` | **6** | the mailbox header |
| `MBX_$SERV_MSG_MAX` | **1030** | 1024 of data plus the 6-byte header |
| `MBX_$VERSION` | **1** | |

**The 1024 is not the ring's.** `MBX_$MSG_MAX` bounds what `MBX_$OPEN` and
`MBX_$TIMED_OPEN` may carry with an open request — "even if the mailbox message
buffer is larger" — while an established channel carries up to 32760 bytes. So
the same number that `IPC_$DATA_T` fixes as a datagram's payload appears here as
a *connection-setup* limit over a service that can otherwise move 32 KB. Two
independent ceilings that happen to share a value, recorded as such rather than
merged.

The message header is the one structure worth having: `MBX_$MSG_HDR_T` is `cnt`
at 0 ("the total number of bytes in the message, **including the header**"), `mt`
at 2 (a `MBX_$MTYPE_T`) and `chan` at 4 — six bytes, matching
`MBX_$SERV_MSG_HDR_LEN`; and `MBX_$SERVER_MSG_T` is that header with up to 1024
data bytes at offset 6, totalling `MBX_$SERV_MSG_MAX`. **Servers and clients have
different message formats**: "a server must include the 6-byte message header
when sending a message. In contrast, a client sends only data" — which is why
every `buflen` in the chapter is 32766 for a server and 32760 for a client,
differing by exactly the header.

The message types are the protocol: `MBX_$CHANNEL_OPEN_MT` (a client asking for a
channel), `MBX_$ACCEPT_OPEN_MT` and `MBX_$REJECT_OPEN_MT` (the server's answer),
`MBX_$DATA_MT`, `MBX_$DATA_PARTIAL_MT` and `MBX_$EOF_MT`.

### `TIME_$CLOCKH_T` used as a duration

`MBX_$TIMED_OPEN`'s `wait-time` is "time to wait for an open reply, **in
`TIME_$CLOCKH_T` format**", zero meaning wait forever. That is the fourth chapter
to use the 4-byte top half of the 48-bit 4 µs clock — `TIME` defines it, `CAL`
gives the epoch, `IOS` uses it for file time stamps, and here it is a *relative*
interval. One type, absolute and relative, at 262,144 µs a tick.

Note the contrast with `IPC`, whose timeouts are a 2-byte count of
quarter-seconds. Two IPC mechanisms in the same manual, two different time units
for the same quantity.

### The two eventcounts, and what a full channel does

`MBX_$GET_EC` returns one of two, and the distinction is careful: `GETREC_EC_KEY`
"advances when the mailbox **may** contain messages for you to get", and
`PUTREC_EC_KEY` "advances when there **may** be enough room in the channel to
hold the last message you unsuccessfully tried to put there". The manual then
says why the hedge is there — "a mailbox server sees only **one**
`MBX_$PUTREC_EC_KEY` eventcount for the entire mailbox. If puts fail with
`MBX_$NO_ROOM_IN_CHANNEL` on two channels of the same mailbox, the event's
completion simply says that **at least one** channel may now take the message."
An eventcount is a hint to re-check, not a guarantee, and this is the clearest
statement of that in the manual.

**A full channel blocks; it does not drop.** `MBX_$PUT_REC` "waits until there is
room"; `MBX_$PUT_REC_COND` returns `MBX_$NO_ROOM_IN_CHANNEL` immediately. That is
the opposite of `IPC`, where a full socket queue loses the datagram outright —
the difference between a virtual circuit and a datagram, stated by the two
chapters' own failure modes.

`MBX_$GET_REC` and `MBX_$GET_CONDITIONAL` note that when a server gets a message
"the mailbox manager uses a **scheduling algorithm** to determine the channel to
search for the next message. This algorithm guarantees **fair service** to each
open channel" — named and not described.

### The remote path, and the number 1158

A message to a client on another node passes through "the system mailbox
maintained by the remote node's **`MBX_HELPER`**", whose buffer is set by its
`-DATASIZE` option and defaults to **1158 bytes**. Three pages repeat it, and
MBX-31 gives its other meaning: `MBX_$SERVER_WINDOW` "always returns the value
1158" for a client on a **pre-SR9** node, because 1158 is the minimum buffer a
pre-SR9 `MBX_HELPER` maintains. `MBX_$CLIENT_WINDOW` (MBX-7) has the same caveat
from the other side. So 1158 is simultaneously a default, a minimum, and the
value a version check degrades to — recorded together because meeting any one of
them alone would be misleading. `MBX_$SEQUENCED_SEND_FAILED` is "an internal
error ... while sending a message that is **larger than 1158 bytes**", so the
number also marks where the manager switches to a sequenced path.

### Nothing implementable, and one connection worth keeping

Like `IPC`, this is above the driver: there is no register, no timing and no
device in it. What it is worth is the shape of what runs over the ring once the
ring works — a connection-oriented service with per-channel buffers, a 6-byte
header, a fairness rule and an explicit relay process on each node. The `NAME`
chapter at PDF 329 is the third piece of the same picture and is next but one in
document order.

## `MS` walked whole — the operating system calls 1024 bytes a page, and this machine's `TC` agrees

PDF 265-300, read as images 2026-09-08, 365 of 722. Thirty-three numbered pages.
`MS`, the Mapped Segment manager, is where Domain/OS maps a file into a process's
address space — the layer directly above the 68030's MMU — and it is the first
chapter in this walk to say something a hardware register can be checked against.

### The page is 1024 bytes, said in words

`MS_$TRUNCATE` (MS-31): "when you unmap a file, the system may set the file
length to a **page-aligned** value. (**That is, the length will be a multiple of
1024.**) However, you can use `MS_$TRUNCATE` to shorten the file to a
nonpage-aligned value ... if a file contains only 20 bytes of data, you can use
`MS_$TRUNCATE` to set the file length to 20. When you unmap the file, the length
will be 20 rather than 1024."

**This machine's own `TC` says the same thing.** The identity boot's MMU trace
loads `TC <- 82A28750`, and `[030]` §9.7.1's fields decompose it as

| field | bits | value | meaning |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| `E` | 31 | 1 | translation enabled |
| `SRE` | 25 | 1 | supervisor root pointer used |
| `FCL` | 24 | 0 | no function-code lookup |
| **`PS`** | 23-20 | **`A` = 10** | **page offset is 10 bits — 1024 bytes** |
| `IS` | 19-16 | 2 | two high-order bits ignored |
| `TIA`/`TIB`/`TIC`/`TID` | 15-0 | 8, 7, 5, 0 | three levels, then stop |

and the manual's own consistency rule closes on it: `PS + IS + TIA + TIB + TIC =
10 + 2 + 8 + 7 + 5 = 32`, which is what `ap_m68030_tc_is_consistent` enforces.
`ap_m68030_tc.h` already documents `PS` as "the page offset width in bits", so
`ap_m68030_tc_page_size` returns 1024 for this value with no change needed.

**So three levels of this machine agree on 1024 and each says it differently**:
the MMU's `PS` field as programmed by the running operating system, the file
system's block (`IOS_$INQ_FILE_ATTR`'s "1024-byte blocks", the payload of our
1056-byte AWD sector), and now the mapping manager calling that unit a *page* in
prose. Nothing here changes the model; what it does is turn a decoded register
field into a number the operating system's own documentation states.

### The lock matrix, printed twice

MS-19 (for `MS_$MAPL`) and MS-22/23 (for `MS_$MAPL_STREAM`) print the same two
tables. A lock is a pair — a concurrency mode and an access mode:

| lock | concurrency | access |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Protected Read | `MS_$NR_XOR_1W` | `MS_$R` or `MS_$RX` |
| Protected RIW | `MS_$NR_XOR_1W` | `MS_$RIW` |
| Shared Read | `MS_$COWRITERS` | `MS_$R`, `MS_$RX` or `MS_$RIW` |
| Exclusive Write | `MS_$NR_XOR_1W` | `MS_$WR` or `MS_$WRX` |
| Shared Write | `MS_$COWRITERS` | `MS_$WR` or `MS_$WRX` |

and a compatibility matrix over the five, whose only interesting entries are the
two marked `*`: **Shared Read against Shared Write, and Shared Write against
Shared Write, "are allowed only if the processes are on the same node."** So a
shared write lock is a *local* construct; across the ring the file server will not
grant it. That is a distributed-system fact stated as a table footnote, and it is
the reason `MS_$RELOCK` exists.

`MS_$ACC_MODE_T` has five values — `R`, `RX`, `WR`, `WRX`, `RIW` — so the
operating system's protection model distinguishes **execute** from read, which
neither the 68030's MMU nor the 68851's short-format descriptors carry: `[030]`
§9.6 gives write-protect and supervisor-only and nothing else. The distinction is
enforced above the hardware, by the file system's ACLs, and this is where it lives.

### `MS_$ATTRIB_T` is the VTOC entry's fields, presented

MS-3's attribute record is 22 bytes: `permanent` (bool) at 0, `immutable` (bool)
at 1, `cur_len` at 2, `blocks_used` at 6, then `dtu`, `dtm` and `dtcr` at 10, 14
and 18, **each a `time_$clockh_t`**. `002398-04` §2 p. 56's `vtoce_hdr_t` carries
the same set — current length in bytes, blocks used, date-time last used, date-time
last modified — and its flag byte carries *permanent* and *immutable* as bits. So
this is the VTOC entry seen through the mapping manager, the same relationship
`IOS_DIR`'s entry has to the on-disk directory record: same fields, different
packing, and the identifying half (the object's UID, its ACL's UID) withheld.

That is a **fifth** appearance of `TIME_$CLOCKH_T`, and the third distinct use —
absolute time stamps here and in `IOS`, a relative duration in `MBX`, the
eventcount's own unit in `TIME`.

### Smaller things

- **`MS_$ADVICE` changes the fault-ahead count**: "the system brings pages into
  memory as needed. By using `MS_$ADVICE`, you can change **the number of pages
  that the system gets when a page fault occurs**." `MS_$NORMAL` (the default),
  `MS_$RANDOM`, `MS_$SEQUENTIAL`. Named, not quantified — the manual never says
  how many pages any of the three fetches.
- **Write-back is lazy locally and eager remotely** (MS-32): "if the original
  object is on a **remote** node, changes ... are written back when `MS_$UNMAP` is
  executed. If the original object is on the **local** node, changes ... will be
  written back **when the space they occupy in memory is needed**." A boot's disk
  traffic therefore depends on memory pressure, not on unmap.
- **A force-write also writes the directory** (MS-15): "when you force-write a
  permanent object, the system also force-writes the directory where the object is
  cataloged" — two objects written for one call, which a disk-command census would
  otherwise read as a driver anomaly.
- `MS_$CRTEMP` creates a nameless object *on a named volume*, and `MS_$NEIGHBORS`
  exists solely to ask whether two temporary objects landed on the same one.
- Maximum-length pathnames are 256 characters throughout, as everywhere else in
  this manual.

### Four documentary errors

- **MS-12 and MS-14** name a concurrency mode **`MS_$WR_XOR_1W`**; the type is
  `MS_$NR_XOR_1W` on MS-4, MS-11, MS-13, MS-17 and MS-21. The `NR` is right.
- **MS-16**: `MS_$FW_PARTIAL`'s FORMAT line reads `MS_$FW_FILE (address,
  deired-length, status)` — the wrong call name and a misspelling in one line.
- **MS-19 and MS-22**: the lock table's last row ends "`MS_$WR or MS`", truncating
  `MS_$WRX`. Both printings are cut at the same point, so it is the source and not
  the scan.
- **MS-27**: `MS_$WRX` is glossed "Read, write, and execute access" where MS-2,
  MS-18 and MS-21 all say "Write and execute access". MS-27's is the sensible
  reading and the other three are the repeated one; recorded rather than resolved.

## `MUTEX` and `NAME` walked whole — the 48-bit clock gets a byte count, and the directory entry a third printing

PDF 318-348, read as images 2026-09-08, 396 of 722. Six numbered pages and
twenty, plus three blanks and two dividers.

### `MUTEX` — six pages, and both its data types confirm earlier chapters

The whole chapter is three calls (`$INIT`, `$LOCK`, `$UNLOCK`) over one record,
and it pays entirely through the types it reprints.

**`TIME_$CLOCK_T` is six bytes, stated as a number.** MUTEX-3 reprints the type
the `TIME` chapter defines — `high` (4 bytes) at 0 and `low` (2 bytes) at 4, with
the overlaid `high16`/`low32` view — and MUTEX-5 adds what `TIME` never says
outright: "**This data type is 6 bytes long**". Six bytes is 48 bits, which is
what this project has been asserting about the 4 µs clock since `CAL`, derived
from the 32 + 16 split rather than read. Now it is printed.

**`EC2_$EVENTCOUNT_T` is confirmed from a second chapter.**
`MUTEX_$LOCK_REC_T` is `lock_byte` at 0 (a 2-byte integer holding a Boolean),
then `lock_ec` at 2 — "in `EC2_$EVENTCOUNT` format" — whose `value` sits at 2 and
`awaiters` at 6, and the record totals **8 bytes**. That fixes `value` at 4 bytes
and `awaiters` at 2, which is exactly what the `EC2` section above derived from
"this data type is 6 bytes long" plus the width of the diagram's boxes. Two
chapters, one layout, and this one shows the eventcount *embedded* in another
structure at a stated offset.

`MUTEX_$WAIT_FOREVER` is `integer32(-1)`. And MUTEX-5 has the same node
restriction `MS` gives its shared write lock: "**All programs that map the same
MUTEX lock record must be on the same node**", with the honest caveat that "a
MUTEX lock is a **convention** that cooperating programs use ... if a program does
not use `MUTEX_$LOCK` and accesses the resource directly, you cannot guarantee
mutual exclusion." The lock is advisory; the `MS` lock is not.

### `NAME` — the directory entry a third time, and now with numbers

`NAME_$DIR_ENTRY_T` (NAME-2) is byte for byte `IOS_DIR_$ENTRY_T`: `enttype` at 0,
`entlen` at 2, `entname` at 4 as a 32-character `name_$name_t`, `unused1` at 36,
`unused2` at 40. **What this chapter adds is the constants**:

| constant | value |
| --- | --- |
| `NAME_$COMPLEN_MAX` | **32** — maximum length of an entry name |
| `NAME_$PNAMLEN_MAX` | **256** — maximum length of a pathname |
| **`NAME_$FILE`** | **1** — "the file type value for the `enttype` field" |
| **`NAME_$LINK`** | **3** — "the link type value for the `enttype` field" |

`IOS_DIR` gave the two type codes only as names. **`002398-04` §2 p. 39 gives them
as numbers from the disk side — `0` not in use, `1` name/UID pair, `3`
name/link-data pair — and they agree.** So the entry type's *values* are now
confirmed across the two levels, and the 44-byte width is confirmed a third way:
NAME-11 says `NAME_$ENTRY_T` "is 44 bytes long" and describes the FORTRAN view as
"a **(22,n) INTEGER\*2 array**" — 22 two-byte integers is 44 bytes per column.

**A contradiction inside the chapter, resolved 2 against 1.** NAME-11's
`NAME_$EXTRACT_DATA` lists the entry types as "**2** - `NAME_$LINK`". NAME-2's
constants table says **3**, and `002398-04` says 3. NAME-11 is the error. Recorded
because a reader who met only that page would build a directory walker that
misclassifies every link.

`NAME_$DIR_LIST_T` is "a **1300-element** array of `NAME_$DIR_ENTRY_T`", i.e.
57,200 bytes — an odd ceiling, printed without explanation, and the only place the
manual bounds a directory read.

### The naming server's own error list, which is module `0E`

NAME-20 lists them, alphabetically and without codes: `ALREADY_EXISTS`,
`BAD_DIRECTORY`, `BAD_LEAF`, `BAD_LINK`, `BAD_PATHNAME`, `DIRECTORY_FULL`,
`DIRECTORY_NOT_EMPTY`, `FILE_NOT_DIRECTORY`, `ILL_LINK_OP`,
`INSUFFICIENT_RIGHTS`, **`IS_SYSBOOT`** ("unable to delete system bootstrap
(sysboot)"), `NO_RIGHTS`, `NODE_UNAVAILABLE`, `NOT_FILE`, **`NOT_FOUND` ("Name
not found")** and `NOT_LINK`.

**This is the error set of the module behind `E0007`.** `002398-04` p. 73's module
`0E` table prints `(000E0007) name not found`, `not a link`, `invalid link
operation`, `bad directory` — and every one of those four is in this list, word
for word. Two manuals, one module, and the identification of module `0E` as the
naming server is now made from both ends.

**What it does *not* give is the numbering.** The list is alphabetical, so no
ordinal can be read off it, and inferring "`NOT_FOUND` is the seventh entry" from
an alphabetical list would be exactly the kind of numeric coincidence this
project has been wrong about before. `002398-04` §4 remains the only source that
pairs a name with its code.

*Smaller things*: a pathname beginning with `/` is placed in "the **entry
directory of the local node**", said on eight separate pages; `NAME_$SET_NDIR`
accepts a bare period to set the naming directory equal to the working directory;
and `NAME_$READ_DIR`'s index is a 4-byte key that the call **rewrites** for the
next call, with the warning that "to get an accurate snapshot of a directory, make
only **one** call ... because the contents of a directory can change between
calls."

*A documentary error*: NAME-5's `NAME_$CNAME` transposes the two limits —
`old-pathname` in `NAME_$PNAME_T` format is called "an array of up to **32**
characters" and `new-leaf` in `NAME_$NAME_T` format "up to **256**". The
constants on NAME-2 and NAME-3 have them the other way round, and every other call
in the chapter agrees with the constants.

## `PAD` walked whole — the pixel pitch, what the off-screen memory holds, and three keyboards

PDF 349-408, read as images 2026-09-08, 456 of 722. Fifty-seven numbered pages of
Display Manager calls. Most of it is window management with nothing below it, and
four things in it are hardware facts no source held here had.

### The display census, printed a second time — and that is all it is

`PAD_$INQ_DISP_TYPE` (PAD-29) returns the **same eight values** the `SMD` chapter
gives, with the same dimensions: `NONE`, `BW_15P` portrait, `BW_19L` landscape,
`COLOR_DISPLAY` 1024×1024, `800_COLOR` 1024×800, `COLOR2_DISPLAY` 1280×1024×8,
`COLOR3_DISPLAY` 1024×800×8, `COLOR4_DISPLAY` 1024×800×4. The section above
already records that list from `SMD_$INQ_DISP_TYPE` and the mapping onto
`ap_graphics.h`'s part numbers.

**Two chapters of one manual is corroboration of a printing, not a second
witness** — the rule this walk record already applies to `STATUS_$T`. What it
does buy is that the eight-entry census is not a typesetting accident in one
chapter.

*And it exposes a stale type definition.* `PAD_$DISPLAY_TYPE_T` on PAD-3 lists
only **five** values — `BW_15P`, `BW_19L`, `COLOR_DISPLAY`, `800_COLOR`, `NONE`
— where the call that returns the type lists eight. The three `COLORn` entries
were added to the call and not to the type. A reader who trusts the Data Types
section alone builds a display model missing every board with a stated depth.

### What the off-screen memory is for, said outright

`PAD_$LOAD_FONT` (PAD-40): "PAD_$LOAD_FONT does not switch fonts. It merely loads
the font into the **invisible portion of display memory** and returns a font ID."
Up to **100 fonts** per pad.

`ap_graphics.h` derives each board's buffer width from `008778-03`'s printed
memory capacities — a 4-plane board's 128 KB plane is 1024 × 1024 bits with
1024 × 800 visible — and says a model using the visible width as the stride
shears the image. **This says what the extra 224 lines hold**: a glyph cache the
Display Manager blits from. Recorded in that file, because a dump of display RAM
compared against the oracle's will contain fonts, and a model that treats the
region as slack is wrong about the contents while being right about the screen.

### The pixel pitch, and the raster unit

- **PAD-54**: "the display contains approximately **100 bits per inch**". A
  three-element tab array of 100, 300, 500 sets stops "about 1, 3, and 5 inches
  (2.54, 7.62, and 12.70 cm) from the left edge". **This is the only pixel pitch
  any source in this project gives.** At 100 dpi, 1024 pixels is 10.2 inches in
  each direction — consistent with the 15-inch diagonal `PAD_$BW_15P` is named
  for.
- **PAD-53**: "One raster unit is equal to **one bit in the display**." Which is
  `ap_graphics.h`'s pixel-per-plane-bit model stated from the software side.
  Scale factors multiply into raster units; a scale of zero means "use the
  current font's size", so an application's coordinates are in characters and
  lines by default.

Both are now in `ap_graphics.h` beside the geometry they qualify.

### Three keyboards, discriminated by a filename suffix

`PAD_$INQ_KBD` (PAD-35) returns a **suffix** to append to a Display Manager
pathname to find the right key-definition file:

| suffix | keyboard |
| --- | --- |
| null string | the **880** keyboard |
| `"2"` | the **low-profile** keyboard |
| `"3"` | the low-profile keyboard **with numeric keypad** |

with the paths `/SYS/DM/STD_KEYS` and `USER_DATA/KEY_DEFS`. `ap_kbd.h` models one
keyboard, from `002398-04` ch. 13 and the identification string `FF 12 21` the
part answers with; this is the operating system's own three-way distinction and
the file to look in if a key ever decodes wrongly. Recorded rather than acted on:
nothing in this core's boot depends on which of the three it is, and inventing a
second keyboard model to match a suffix would be a change with no measurement
behind it.

### The cursor position report, byte by byte

PAD-13 gives the format a raw-mode input pad interleaves with character data,
which is a wire format a console capture could meet:

    1 byte   PAD_$CPR_FLAG (16#FF) -- the next 5 bytes are a report
    2 bytes  x coordinate
    2 bytes  y coordinate
    1 byte   the raw keystroke, or PAD_$NO_KEY (16#FE) if none,
             or PAD_$LEFT_WINDOW (16#FD) if the cursor left the window

Coordinates are scaled and relative to the frame's top-left corner. Five report
modes: `NONE` (default), `CHANGE`, `ALL`, `PICK` (after the cursor settles from
the touchpad, bitpad or mouse) and `DRAW` (every position during movement) — the
last two naming the same three locator devices `TPAD_$DEV_TYPE_T` does.

### Ceilings, and other small facts

**40 pads and 60 windows** per node (PAD-16); **10** windows in a
`PAD_$WINDOW_LIST_T`; **100** fonts per pad; **100** tab stops
(`PAD_$MAX_TABSTOPS`), default every 4 columns; a frame is up to **32767** raster
units in each direction; an input pane is at least 1 line deep and 20 is "a common
relative size". The control characters are ASCII throughout — `BS` 8, `TAB` 9,
`NEWLINE` 10, `FF` 12, `CR` 13, `ESCAPE` 27, the last "precedes ANSI escape
sequences". Raw mode "clears the input pad and shrinks its window size to zero",
and a program using `PAD_$RAW` **must** call `PAD_$COOKED` before terminating
because "most system utilities, including the Shell, will not work correctly in
raw mode".

*A documentary inconsistency*: `PAD_$KEY_DEF_T` is "up to **256** characters" on
PAD-3 and the same parameter is "up to **128** characters" on PAD-22.

## `PBUFS` and `PFM` walked whole — how the operating system classifies a 68030 exception

PDF 409-430, read as images 2026-09-08, 478 of 722. Four numbered pages and
fourteen.

### `PBUFS` — four pages, nothing here

Two calls, `$CREATE` and `$OPEN`, over files in `/sys/node_data/paste_buffers`;
a buffer name is a **full 32-byte `NAME_$NAME_T` padded with blanks**, which is
the entry-name width the `NAME` chapter fixes. A Boolean chooses text or a **GMF**
buffer holding images, and the one error is `PBUFS_$WRONG_TYPE`. Derived and
empty.

### `PFM` — the synchronous/asynchronous split, named against real exceptions

The finding is one sentence on PFM-9, and it is about the parts this core models:

> "Inhibiting asynchronous faults has no effect on the processing of
> **synchronous faults such as floating-point overflow errors, access violations,
> address errors**, and so on."

So Domain/OS's fault taxonomy maps onto the 68030's exception vectors: an
**address error** (vector 3), an **access violation** — a bus error from the MMU,
vector 2 — and an **FPCP overflow** (vector 53) are *synchronous* and reach the
program whatever `PFM_$INHIBIT` is doing. What `$INHIBIT` gates is the
asynchronous kind, whose named example is "the Display Manager quit (DQ) command
that occurs when someone types a CTRL/Q" — a keystroke, not a trap.

**And the queue depth is one.** PFM-5 and PFM-9 both state it: "while faults are
inhibited, the operating system holds **at most one** asynchronous fault ... if
more than one fault occurred while faults were inhibited, the program receives
the **first**." A held fault is kept, later ones are dropped, and the one kept is
the earliest.

The rest is the handler machinery, recorded as context: handlers are established
per fault type with `PFM_$ALL_FAULTS` (= 0) as the catch-all;
`PFM_$FH_MULTI_LEVEL` extends a handler to subordinate program levels and
`PFM_$FH_BACKSTOP` runs it after every non-backstop handler; a handler returns
`PFM_$CONTINUE_FAULT_HANDLING` to pass the fault on or
`PFM_$RETURN_TO_FAULTING_CODE` to resume. Clean-up handlers are a **LIFO stack**
with a built-in one always at the bottom that "closes any files that are still
open and returns control to the invoking Shell" — which is what a program's exit
path looks like from below. `PFM_$SIGNAL` "does not return to the place from
which it was called" and stores **no** traceback; `PFM_$ERROR_TRAP` is the same
thing *with* traceback, and "when a fault occurs the operating system
automatically stores traceback information".

`PFM_$FAULT_REC_T` is a 2-byte `pattern` ("reserved for PFM use") followed by a
`STATUS_$T` at offset 2 — six bytes, and the fifth printing of the status
longword's field boundaries in this walk.

*A documentary contradiction*: `PFM_$CLEANUP_REC` is "**64 bytes** long" on PFM-2
and "**8 bytes** long" on PFM-4, PFM-11 and PFM-12. Three pages against one, so 8
is the reading; recorded because a caller who allocates 8 from the data-types
page would be right and one who allocates 64 would merely waste stack.

## `PGM`, `PM` and `PRF` walked whole — the four standard streams, numbered

PDF 431-482, read as images 2026-09-08, 530 of 722. Twenty-three numbered pages,
four and eighteen.

### `PGM` — and it closes a small loop the `ERROR` chapter opened

**The standard streams are numbered outright** (PGM-14): "by default, every
program is invoked with four streams, numbered 0 through 3. **Stream 0 is
standard input, stream 1 is standard output, Stream 2 is error input, stream 3
is error output.**" The `ERROR` chapter gave `STREAM_$ERROUT` = 3 and nothing
else; here are all four, and "error *input*" as a distinct stream is the one a
Unix-shaped expectation would not predict.

**`UID_$T`'s layout is printed** (PGM-6): `high` at offset 0 and `low` at 4, four
bytes each — the eight bytes `IOS` states as a width and `002398-04` §2's canned
UIDs are printed in. *A documentary error*: the second field's description is
headed **`high`** and reads "the **low** four bytes of the UID".

**`EC2_$EVENTCOUNT_T` a third time** (PGM-2), `value` at 0 and `awaiters` at 4 —
and this printing glosses `awaiters` as "**first process waiting**" where `EC2`
called it "reserved for internal use by the EC2 manager". The same field, named
from inside.

*The rest*: severity levels `OK`/`TRUE` = 0, `FALSE` = 1, `WARNING` = 2, `ERROR`
= 3, `OUTPUT_INVALID` = 4, `INTERNAL_FATAL` = 5, `PROGRAM_FAULTED` = 6,
`MAX_SEVERITY` = 15, and `PGM_$EXIT` "calls `PFM_$SIGNAL` with a status code
equal to the last severity level set" — which is how the `PFM` chapter's LIFO
clean-up stack gets entered on a normal exit. Arguments are numbered from 0, with
0 the program name; `PGM_$ARG` is a 2-byte length followed by up to 128
characters; the argument vector holds up to 128 pointers. `PGM_$INVOKE` runs a
program in the same process (`PGM_$WAIT`), as a child whose status comes back
(the default, a null set), or as an **orphan** (`PGM_$BACK_GROUND`) with no
status at all; a child's eventcount is **0 while running and 1 at termination**.

*Documentary defects*: `PGM_$BACKGROUND` on PGM-4 is `PGM_$BACK_GROUND` on PGM-15
and PGM-18; PGM-14 says "you are permitted to pass up to **32** streams" and in
the next paragraph that the connection vector permits "**up to 128 elements**",
with `PGM_$BAD_CONNV` glossed "stream vector too large (>32)"; and PGM-23's error
list prints `PGM_$PROGRAM_FAULTE`, truncating the `D`.

### `PM` — four pages, and the login identifier's printed form

`PM_$GET_SID_TXT` returns the SID "in the form `person.group.project.nodeid`",
up to 140 characters — and describes those components in the preceding sentence
as "the person, **project, organization** and node ID", which does not match its
own format string. The format is the one Domain/OS actually prints, so `group` is
the reading. `PM_$GET_HOME_TXT` returns the home directory, "obtained from the
network registry when you log in and inherited by all your processes", up to 256.
Nothing else in the chapter; PM-1 says outright that "the PM calls do not use
produce unique error messages" — a sentence with a word too many in it.

### `PRF` — a print spooler, and one line that touches the display

Eighteen pages of print-queue calls: a *print request packet* in
`/sys/print/queue` and, optionally, a copy of the data in `/sys/print/spooler`,
consumed by a print server called **PRSVR**. The option list is the chapter's
substance and it is entirely printer-side — copies, margins, headers, pitch,
point size, PostScript on or off, paper sizes A/B/LEGAL/A3/A4/A5/B4/B5.

One option is worth recording beside `ap_graphics.h`: **`BW_REV ON|OFF` —
"enable/disable black and white reversal for bitmaps", default OFF.** The `GMF`
chapter established that in a graphics map file "'1' bits mean black", and a
screen dump printed without reversal therefore comes out with the screen's own
polarity. `PLOT ON` is what selects bitmap mode "to print bitmap files created by
a graphics metafile (GMF) manager or GPR or the **CPSCR (COPY_SCREEN)**
command" — so the path from this core's frame buffer to paper is GMF, and the
manual names the command that makes one.

`NUM_VAR_T` is a 4-byte variant — `real32`, `int32`, a padded `int16`, a padded
`int8`, or a Boolean in bit 0 — which is how one option carries any of five value
types. Nothing else here is implementable, and the chapter's whole error list is
`STATUS_$OK`.

## `PROC1`, `PROC2` and `RWS` walked whole — the 4 µs clock a fourth time, and A6 named

PDF 483-518, read as images 2026-09-08, 566 of 722. Three numbered pages, nine
and sixteen. **`PROC1` is 3 pages, not the 8 the chapter map gave** — a third
length correction, and the divider (PDF 483) says so before the chapter starts.

### `PROC1` — three pages, and the fourth printing of the 4 µs unit

One call, `PROC1_$GET_CPUT`, returning "the amount of CPU time used by this
process since its creation, in `TIME_$CLOCK_T` format ... **The returned clock
value has a resolution of 4 microseconds.**"

The `CAL` chapter gives the unit and the 1980 epoch, `TIME` gives the 262,144 µs
eventcount, `MUTEX` gives the type's 6-byte size, and this gives the resolution
again in a completely different context — *CPU time accounting*, not wall time.
So the 4 µs period is the machine's single time quantum and not a calendar
artefact. The chapter also defines what it counts: "the time during which the
process is running in the CPU ... includes the time that the operating system is
performing services for the process, but **does not include the time that the
process spends waiting for I/O transfers to complete**."

### `PROC2` — the per-process register save area, and A6 is the frame pointer

`PROC2_$INFO_T` (PROC2-2) is 36 bytes and is the operating system's view of a
suspended process:

| offset | field | what |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0 | `stack_uid` | `uid_$t`, the user stack's UID (8 bytes) |
| 8 | `stack_base` | base address of the user stack |
| 12 | `state` | `proc2_$state_t` — waiting, suspended, susp-pending, bound |
| 14 | `usr` | **user status register** |
| 16 | `upc` | **user program counter** |
| 20 | `usp` | **user stack pointer** |
| 24 | `usb` | **user stack base pointer (A6)** |
| 28 | `cpu_total` | `time_$clockh_t`, cumulative CPU time |
| 34 | `priority` | |

and PROC2-6 spells the two address registers out: "the user stack pointer
(**A7**)" and "the stack base pointer (**A6**)". So Domain/OS's calling
convention puts the frame pointer in A6 and the stack in A7, which is the
68000-family convention and is here stated by the operating system rather than
assumed. `usr` and `upc` are the two fields a debugger reads, and this is the
record a `PROC2_$GET_INFO` returns — the same quantities this core's identity
report prints for its own final state.

*Note the asymmetry*: "if the process-uid in the call is the caller's own
process, the only information returned is the stack UID and virtual address" —
a process cannot read its own saved registers, because they are not saved while
it runs.

### `RWS` — one address space, shared by every process

The finding is a sentence printed four times (RWS-2, RWS-6, RWS-10, RWS-14):

> "The global pool (`RWS_$GLOBAL_POOL`) makes storage accessible to all
> processes. Note that **pointers are valid in *all* processes because they
> reserve the *identical* portion of address space**."

That is a statement about how the MMU is used: Domain/OS does not give each
process a private address space that happens to overlap — the global region is at
**the same virtual addresses in every process**, which is why a raw pointer can be
passed between them. `RWS-7`'s table adds that global-pool storage is "kept until
**reboot**", outliving every process.

**What this is and is not evidence for.** This core's identity boot loads
`SRP` and `CRP` with the *same* value (`80000002 01200000`), which is the same
shape of arrangement — one table serving both. But that boot runs the **PROM's
self-test**, not Domain/OS, so it is a consistency observation and not a
measurement of what this chapter describes. Recorded that way deliberately.

The three pools are `STD` (calling process only), `GLOBAL` (all processes, until
reboot) and `STREAM_TM` (survives a UNIX `EXEC`). Overheads are printed and then
disclaimed: heap costs "about 16 bytes" from the standard and stream pools and
"about 4 bytes" from the global one, read/write storage costs nothing, and a NOTE
says "do not depend on the exact amount". Four of the eight calls are marked
**obsolete** in the manual's own italics — `$ALLOC_HEAP`, `$ALLOC_RW`,
`$RELEASE_HEAP` and, by implication, their pool-less pattern — replaced by the
`_POOL` forms. `RWS_$ALLOC` is the only one FORTRAN can call, "due to FORTRAN
calling conventions".

The error list is the interesting half: `RWS_$SCRIBBLED_OVER` ("user program
wrote over the storage where the system stored the heap's process information")
and `RWS_$LEVEL_FAILURE` (the same for program-level information) — so the heap's
bookkeeping lives *in* the allocated region and a buffer overrun is detected by
finding it damaged.

### Documentary defects

- **PROC2-7**: `PROC2_$UID_LIST_T` is "a 24-element array of UIDs. Each UID is a
  **4-byte** integer in `UID_$T` format." `UID_$T` is 8 bytes on PROC2-5, PROC2-8,
  PGM-6 and IOS-9. The 4 is wrong.
- **PROC2-5** refers to `PROC1_$CPU_TIME`; the call is `PROC1_$GET_CPUT`.
- **RWS-1**: "The RWS (Read/Write Storage) programming **allow** programs to
  allocate ..." — a missing word, in the same position as `PM`'s "do not use
  produce unique error messages".

## `STREAMS` walked whole — the file-attribute record, and a VTOC bit surfacing as a warning

PDF 584-646, read as images 2026-09-08, 630 of 722. Sixty numbered pages — the
map's figure, confirmed. `STREAMS` is the layer `IOS` sits on, and it is the
fullest single account this manual gives of what a Domain/OS file *is*.

### The 44-byte directory entry, a fourth time — and a type label that is wrong

`STREAM_$DIR_ENTRY_T` (STREAM-11) is `enttype` at 0, `entlen` at 2, `entname` at
4, `unused1` at 36, `unused2` at 40 — byte for byte `IOS_DIR_$ENTRY_T` and
`NAME_$DIR_ENTRY_T`, with `enttype` "either `NAME_$FILE` or `NAME_$LINK`".

**Its type label is a documentary error.** The diagram brackets `entname` as
`name_$pname_t`, the **256**-character pathname type; the offsets on the same
diagram run 4 to 35, which is **32** bytes, and `IOS_DIR` and `NAME` both call it
`name_$name_t`. The offsets are right and the label is wrong. Recorded because a
reader who trusts the label allocates eight times the record.

### `STREAM_$IR_REC_T` — the whole attribute record at named offsets

STREAM-8 to STREAM-11 give it as a diagram and STREAM-34 to STREAM-39 give the
same fields as prose: `strid`, `obj_namlen`, `rec_lgth`, two flag words, the
header length, the file length, the 12-byte `seek_key`, the current record length
and relative record number, `blks_used`, then **`dtu` and `dtm` as
`time_$clockh_t`**, **`otype` as a `uid_$t`**, two more flag words and the name.

That is `MS_$ATTRIB_T`'s set again, and `002398-04` §2 p. 56's `vtoce_hdr_t`
again, with the flags spelled out bit by bit. Three chapters and one hardware
manual describing one record from four sides, and every one of them puts the two
time stamps in the 4-byte type that ticks every 262,144 µs.

**And it names two things Domain/OS does not implement**, in its own words:
`STREAM_$CONC`, object concurrency, is marked "(not implemented)" in both the
`$INQUIRE` and `$REDEFINE` masks, and Flag1's bits 14-15 say "**Not implemented.
Always is `STREAM_$N_AND_N`**" — any number of readers and writers. So the
five-value concurrency type is aspirational and the file system enforces nothing
at that level in January 1987.

### The object-type UID census — a serial line and a tape are files

STREAM-38 and STREAM-49 print the valid `otype` values: `UASC_$UID` (text),
`RECORDS_$UID`, `HDR_$UNDEF_$UID`, `OBJECT_FILE_$UID`, `PAD_$UID` (a saved
transcript), `INPUT_PAD_$UID`, **`SIO_$UID`** (serial line descriptor),
`MBX_$UID`, **`MT_$UID`** (magnetic tape descriptor). `IOS` gives the same list;
`002398-04` §2 p. 50 gives the *mechanism* — canned UIDs like `pv_label_$uid`
`00000200,0` — and this project used it to identify blocks on the installed
volume. These are the same kind of constant for device objects.

`STREAM_$SIO_NOT_LOCAL`, "SIO object not in **/DEV**", names where they live.

### A VTOC bit surfacing as a user-visible warning

`STREAM_$FILE_TROUBLE_WARNING` (STREAM-58): "**WARNING: (SALVAGER) File trouble
bit set in VTOCE.**"

`002398-04` §2 p. 56's `vtoce_hdr_t` flag byte has **`F` "file needs salvaging"
(`.trouble`)**, recorded in that walk as part of the per-object record. Here is
what a program sees when it opens a file with that bit set. An on-disk flag and
the string a user reads, joined — and a reminder that a volume this project
salvages by hand has a per-*file* trouble bit as well as the BAT header's
per-*volume* `V`.

### What a console driver gets by default

Twice stated (STREAM-16, STREAM-42): "For streams to **SIO lines**, however,
'**cooked**' input mode and **NO_WAIT** are always in effect when the stream is
opened." So a program opening a serial line gets line-buffered input that never
blocks, and must call `STREAM_$REDEFINE` or `SIO_$CONTROL` to change it — which
is the `SIO` chapter's `RAW` option seen from the stream side, and the reason
`STREAM_$GET_CONDITIONAL` exists at all: "performs read operations on streams
such as **SIO lines**, input pads, and mailboxes, for which data may not yet be
available".

*And the text-file convention*: "UASC file records are delimited by the
**line-feed character (16#0A)**" — LF, not CR/LF, on a Domain/OS volume.
`STREAM_$PUT_REC` "never inserts newline characters in the object on its own".

*Smaller things*: `STREAM_$MAX` is **127**, matching `IOS_$MAX`; records are
**word-aligned**, so a full-record read returns an even `retptr`; a negative
`retlen` means the record was longer than the buffer and its absolute value is
what remains; `STREAM_$MAKE_BACKUP` is the `.BAK` mechanism, creating the new
file *on the same volume as the old* and renaming at close; and the eventcount a
stream advances is per keystroke in raw mode, per carriage return in cooked —
the third statement of that rule in this manual.

### Documentary defects

- **STREAM-5 vs STREAM-34/39**: `STREAM_$INQUIRE_MASK_T` and
  `STREAM_$REDEF_MASK_T` are "a **2-byte** integer" in Data Types and "a
  **4-byte** integer" at both calls. Four is right — the masks use bit 26.
- **STREAM-5**: the first value of `STREAM_$CC_T` is printed as
  **`STREAM_$CC_T`**, the type's own name, and the second as `STREAM_$F&&_CC`.
  STREAM-36 and STREAM-48 give the real pair: **`STREAM_$APOLLO_CC`** and
  **`STREAM_$F77_CC`**. Resolved from within the chapter.
- **STREAM-4** lists a concurrency value `STREAM_$REGULATED` "allows unrestricted
  reading and writing"; STREAM-15, 18, 21, 41, 37 and 49 all call it
  **`STREAM_$UNREGULATED`**. The `UN` is right and the gloss proves it.
- **STREAM-11**: `flags4` is a 2-byte field whose table lists "Bit 25" and "Bit
  26". Those are the *mask* bit numbers from STREAM-3, not positions in the word;
  STREAM-39 renumbers the same three as bits 29, 30, 31.
- **STREAM-6** gives `STREAM_$EC_KEY_T` two values; **STREAM-29** says "the only
  value allowed is `STREAM_$GETREC_EC_KEY`". The second key,
  `STREAM_$EDIT_WAIT_EC_KEY`, is defined and never usable.
- `STREAM_$FILE_LGTH` (STREAM-2) is `STREAM_$FILE_LENGTH` (STREAM-34).

## `VEC`, `VFMT` and the front matter — 722 of 722, and the document is finished

PDF 671-722 and 1-14, read as images 2026-09-08. This closes the walk.

### `VEC` — a software BLAS, and the one line that touches this project

Thirty-six pages, every one the same template: a routine, its four type variants
and a FORTRAN loop that *is* the specification. `VEC-1` gives the naming rule —
`VEC_$name` single-precision, `VEC_$Dname` double, `VEC_$Iname` 32-bit integer,
`VEC_$Iname16` 16-bit, and a trailing `_I` for the incremental form that steps by
something other than 1 — so 33 entries in the table of contents are about 130
callable routines.

**The one connection to this core's subject**: `VEC_$MAT_MULT`, `VEC_$POSTMULT`
and `VEC_$PREMULT` are fixed at **4 × 4** and are twice described as "intended for
use in **graphics applications**". So Domain/OS's graphics transform pipeline is
4 × 4 homogeneous matrices computed **in software** — there is no transform
hardware on any board in `ap_graphics.h`'s census, and this is the library that
stands in for it. Recorded as context; nothing here is implementable.

Also worth having: matrices are **FORTRAN column-major** throughout, with a NOTE
that C and Pascal callers "may need to transpose"; and the routines "do **not**
check index values for validity" for performance, "hence, passing a value of zero
can cause a variety of errors". *Documentary defects*: VEC-6's loop initialises
`J` and `K` and uses an uninitialised `L`; VEC-30's initialises none of the three;
`VEC_$SUM_I` is printed as `VEC_SUM_I`, without the `$`, in both the table of
contents and its own heading; VEC-9's and VEC-31's loops accumulate into a `TEMP`
and a `SUM` that are never zeroed; and VEC-10's `inc2` is glossed "increment for
the index of **vec1**" where it is vec2's.

### `VFMT` — twelve pages, and the type `ERROR` depends on

The Variable Text Format package: `$DECODE`/`$ENCODE` over a caller's buffer,
`$READ`/`$WRITE` over standard input and output, `$RS`/`$WS` over a stream.
`VFMT_$STRING_T` is **up to 200 characters**, and **any one call writes at most
512 bytes, silently truncating beyond it**. Each call comes in `2`, `5` and `10`
variants naming the maximum number of arguments, and `$ENCODE`, `$WRITE` and
`$WS` also have a bare form taking up to **20**.

The `ERROR` chapter's forward dependency is closed: `ERROR_$PRINT_FORMAT` and
`ERROR_$STD_FORMAT` take a VFMT control string, and the control-string *syntax*
is not in this manual at all — every one of the seven calls points at "the VFMT
chapter of the **`Programming With General System Calls`** handbook, order no.
005506". That handbook is not held here, and the gap is named rather than left
implicit: **this project cannot construct a VFMT control string from any document
it has**, and has no reason to.

`STATUS_$T` is reprinted for the last time, identical again.

- **PREFACE-1** also explains a hole in INTRO-2's census: "the **GM** and **GPR**

### What the whole document cost and what it gave

722 pages. Nine device chapters carried almost all of the hardware yield — the
4 µs clock's citation, the 262,144 µs tick in print, the baud-partner table, the
FPCR/FPSR third printing, the display census, the palette sizes, the tape
geometry, the beeper's duration type. The remaining 562 gave **six**: the page
size (`MS`), the pixel pitch and the font cache (`PAD`), the 1024-byte block and
`TIME_$CLOCKH_T` as a file time stamp (`IOS`), the 44-byte directory entry with
its type codes (`IOS_DIR`, `NAME`, `STREAMS`), the VTOC trouble bit's message
(`STREAMS`), and `[MAC]`'s "typically 1024 bytes" explained (`IPC`). Every one is
a cross-check on something this core already had, and not one of them changed the
model — which is the outcome the whole-document rule exists to *establish* rather
than to assume.

1. ~~**What is SIO line 0?**~~ **CLOSED 2026-08-22, from a document already
